\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What the Principal Connotation Space Tells
    Us}\label{subsec:what_pcs_tells}

\paragraph{Connotation lives in relations.}
The first thing the space tells us is where the \emph{principal
    connotation} exists: in the relations among works. Miles Davis and
Debussy are joined by one connotation, yet not by any affinity the
pair holds on its own---neither in their descriptors nor in their
sound. They are joined because the position Miles Davis occupies
within jazz is homologous to the position Debussy occupies within
classical music. This homology of standing is what the transport
means by connotation, and it is a conclusion deduced from
independence (A1) and proximity (A2) alone
(Section~\ref{subsec:two_genres}). A name is attached to one work
at a time; connotation is a relation among many. Some things are
defined through naming; others exist beyond naming, defined only
through relations. Connotation is of the second kind.

\paragraph{The boundary of naming is itself the foundation.}
The second thing the space tells us is how the connotation space
was built: on the boundary of naming. Whatever descriptors a record
carries, there always remains a residue---something of the work
beyond them. That boundary served as the foundation: the residue is
given a definite place, the orthogonal complement of what the
denotation accounts for, and from it the principal connotation
space is carved (Section~\ref{subsec:bundle_reach}). The
construction repeats what the bundle drew: fibers standing on a
base. Here too, both kinds are affirmed. There are things naming
attains; and precisely because naming has its limits, a foothold
exists toward the rest. The path beyond naming begins exactly
where naming ends.

\paragraph{Generic representation and descriptors, together.}
The third thing the space tells us is what the construction needed:
a general-purpose metric on the content data. A large language
model can serve any purpose because it holds a generic encoding
within; and the arrival of such encodings---CLAP for sound likewise
(Section~\ref{subsec:content_data})---means that a general-purpose
metric became available: the similarity of two works, measurable as
an inner product. Such a representation arrives entangled: genre
and everything else are woven into one geometry, and separating
them requires a criterion (Section~\ref{subsec:disentanglement}).
That is the role the descriptors play. Naming returns here in its
full strength: it is the criterion by which the cut is made. It
takes both---a generic representation to measure with, and
descriptors to cut by---for the principal connotation space to
stand as an orthogonal subspace.

\bigskip
\noindent
Three statements, one space. Naming gives the foundation and the
criterion, content data gives the metric---and together, for the
first time, they reach beyond naming.

\subsection{Reading Connotation from
    Preference}\label{subsec:reading_preference}

\paragraph{Connotation found in relations with people.}
So far the content data have been the work itself or a description
of it, but nothing requires this. What the OT-FB asks of content
data is one thing: that the proximity between works be measurable
(Section~\ref{subsec:content_data}). Preference data gathered from
enough people meet the condition, and can serve, in principle, just
as well~\cite{Koren200930,Oord20132643}. The reading changes
accordingly. In Section~\ref{subsec:music_library} each region of
the connotation space was read through words---which tags gather
there; with preference data, the reading is through people---who
gathers there. And the accounting stays the same: tags shared
across genres carried high mutual information with $v$, tags bound
to one genre little; likewise, a user who finds favorites across
genres informs the estimate of $v$, and one who stays within a
single genre informs it little. Either way, connotation is found in
relations: among works, or between works and people.

\paragraph{The rabbit hole as anthologist.}
Users who find their favorites sensitively across genres were
counted, above, as highly informative for the estimate of $v$. The
world has such people---anthologists, who wander across genres and
gather works by a criterion of their
own~\cite{Lee20001106,Riva2017}. A rabbit hole plays
their very role, and $v$ can be read as a measure of
\emph{anthologist-ness}~\cite{Bowker1999}. Anthologists
themselves---on the web, in the world of criticism---have often
carried the role of joining different fields: living rabbit holes.
A library with rabbit holes, then, is a library with librarians and
anthologists.

Return, then, to the reference desk of
Section~\ref{sec:introduction}. The visitor asks for ``something
like Miles Davis''; the librarian serves everything a name can
reach---the recordings, the genre, the trumpeters---and where names
end, she does not stop: she calls the anthologists. Who among them
holds Miles Davis dear? One steps forward, one whose gatherings run
through jazz and far beyond it, and takes the visitor walking. What
once waited weeks for a street pianist now stands at the end of a
corridor.

\subsection{From Representation to
    Systems}\label{subsec:representation_to_systems}

Section~\ref{sec:introduction} said it at the outset: representation
precedes any interface, for no interface can surface what the space
beneath it does not encode. The OT-FB now hands the representation
over, and the question turns around: what interfaces does it open?

The possibilities are many. The most direct realizes the image
itself: the multi-floor library with rabbit holes, offered as a
space to walk. A seeker can also point with a work---\textit{Kind
    of Blue}---and be shown the floor it stands on and the rabbit hole
that opens beneath it. And a third reads the seeker's own words as
content data. The groping description of
Section~\ref{sec:introduction}---``quiet, but with feeling in it;
colors that seem to melt into one another''---is a description like
any other, and the encoder that placed the works
(Section~\ref{subsec:book_library}) gives it, too, a place in the
same space. From there, an AI librarian can point to the nearest
floor, an AI anthologist to the nearest rabbit hole, and both can
say: try walking from here. With a language model at hand, such
staff are readily built.\footnote{We have built such a
    library---AI librarian included---as a web application;
    screenshots are included in the supplementary material.}

How its visitors will walk, and whether serendipity will meet them
on the way, is a question of real interest---and it must wait for
the next work. The library is built; the walking is to come.
