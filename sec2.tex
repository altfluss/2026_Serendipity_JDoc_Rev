\section{Serendipity in Information Systems: Prior Work and Research
  Challenge}\label{sec:serendipity}

Research has long known the scene of Section~\ref{sec:introduction}. It has
studied serendipity, asked what a record can hold, and built systems for the
encounter. This section follows that work to where its reach ends, and names
the step beyond.


%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsection{What Serendipity Is}\label{subsec:serendipity_process}

Horace Walpole coined \textit{serendipity} after \textit{The Three Princes of
    Serendip}, whose heroes made discoveries by a mixture of accident and
sagacity~\cite{The-Three-Princes-of-Serendip,Walpole1754}. Both have stood in
the word ever since: the chance encounter, and the prepared mind that Pasteur
said chance favors~\cite{Pasteur1854}. Serendipity is therefore chance met
halfway by a mind in motion, not luck befalling a passive observer.

Research keeps both. Chance appears in the encounter: the serendipitous item is
\textit{unexpected and relevant}~\cite{Kotkov2016180}, with variations across
the field~\cite{Ziarani2021375,Chen2019,Hasan2023933,Nahm2023}. Activity, in
turn, appears in the seeker: the likelihood of meeting such an item depends on
disposition and engagement~\cite{Foster2003321,Erdelez199926,Makri20142179}. Yet
the two differ in one respect---control. Activity can be cultivated; chance, by
contrast, cannot be commanded. Hence McBirnie drew the same line, asking whether
serendipity can be sought at all~\cite{McBirnie2008600}.

Research adds a third feature: the encounter changes the seeker. It means that
unexpectedness arrives mixed with insight, as Makri and Blandford heard
it~\cite{Makri2012684}---a new way of seeing, a new question, a recognition of
what one had been seeking. Serendipity is accordingly understood as a
\emph{process}: one explores, meets the unexpected yet sought-for, grasps its
significance, and comes away with a valuable
outcome~\cite{McCayPeet20151463,Rubin2011,Busch20241110,Makri2012706}. The find,
then, is the occasion; what matters takes place in the finder~\cite{Merton1948505}.

The visitor of Section~\ref{sec:introduction} shows all three at once: he was
seeking actively, the encounter with Debussy was unplanned, and it clarified
what he sought. The scene also shows what the definitions leave open---how far
his own activity could reach. Active search begins once a need becomes words
the system will take. The visitor's need stops short of that: a need of this
kind cannot in principle be specified by the one who has it~\cite{Belkin198261}.

What he could hand the system, then, were names---jazz, trumpet, the
1950s---and a name carries denotation. His activity could therefore reach that
far and no further. Beyond lay connotation, and there chance took over: the
street piano he had not gone to hear. Hence the pairing can now be stated
exactly: serendipity is \textit{denotatively unexpected and connotatively
    relevant}. Denotation can be actively explored; connotation, on this account,
can only be waited for. But could exploration reach one step further, into the
connotative? That is this paper's question. First, how far has the field
carried denotation and connotation?


%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsection{Denotation and Connotation}\label{subsec:naming}

Denotation is what a name can hold, and the field built a superb instrument for
it. Bibliographic control is a technology of naming: it describes a work by
author, genre, subject, and other facets, from the aims Cutter set for the
catalogue~\cite{Cutter1876} to the facet analysis that now underlies retrieval
well beyond the library~\cite{Broughton200649}. Denotation is therefore what
conventional systems handle best: it can be named, recorded, indexed, and
searched.

Connotation is different. It is the second layer of meaning---what a sign
carries beyond what it plainly denotes~\cite{Rafferty2011283}---and it lives
where a work and a person meet~\cite{Rosenblatt1978}. That meeting place is
what keeps it off the record. Connotation resists naming: what a work
expresses, Svenonius found, has no name and therefore no index
term~\cite{Svenonius1994600}, and the visitor of Section~\ref{sec:introduction}
could offer only \textit{colors that seem to melt into one another}. It answers
to the person, and stays there: Saracevic set a \textit{motivational or
    affective} relevance beside the topical~\cite{Saracevic1996201}, and for
Cosijn and Ingwersen such relevance must be judged from the user's side, not
the assessor's~\cite{Cosijn2000533}. What the visitor seeks therefore remains
within him, past the reach of any record. How, then, can a system hold what no
name states?

Every answer the field has offered is a form of naming. The first makes names
finer: mood itself becomes a label on the
record~\cite{Shatford198639,Saricks1989,Cho2023567}, though the finer the
labels, the harder they are to apply consistently~\cite{Saarti200249}. The
second makes names continuous: Osgood's semantic differential rates a concept
between named poles---good--bad, active--passive---and factor analysis reduces
dozens of such scales to three~\cite{Osgood1957}, a plane of valence and
arousal in its best-known descendant~\cite{Russell1980}. The gain is real: the
space has distance, and a concept holds a position in it. Yet the poles are
chosen in advance, the three axes are named in turn, and what is placed is a
word, not a work. To place a work, the work must first be put into words.

The line runs where the collection draws it: where a collection records mood,
mood is denotation; where it records genre alone, mood is connotation. A
collection carries finitely many names, and they can be listed; what no name
gathers is past counting. A system can therefore cover denotation, while
connotation it can only reach into. How far has it reached? That is the
question of the next section.

\subsection{Serendipity in Information Systems}\label{subsec:systems}

Table~\ref{tab:quadrants} sets the two kinds of attribute against the two active
modes of encounter. \textit{Search} requires the seeker to specify what is
sought; \textit{exploration} lets the seeker move through a structured space
without specifying it in advance. Beside these stands a third mode,
\textit{recommendation}. It has a rich literature of its own on
serendipity~\cite{Kotkov2016180,Ziarani2021375,Shrivastava20231,Kunaver2017,Hasan2023933,Chen2019},
but it is left out of the table: there, works are given to the user rather than
sought. (We take up its relation to this paper in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.)
In the left column the visitor of Section~\ref{sec:introduction} has already
exhausted what search can give him: where denotation ends, search ends with it.
The lower right is where he belongs.

Exploration gives the user a structured space instead of a query: the
user moves, and the system provides the
terrain~\cite{McCayPeet20181,Marchionini200641,Thudt20121461,Beale2007ambient}---and
serendipity, we saw, is insight met by a mind in motion. The physical
library has always excelled here. Bj\"orneborn, studying serendipity in
physical libraries, named the affordances that make it so:
\emph{diversifiability}, \emph{traversability}, and
\emph{sensoriability}~\cite{Bjorneborn20171053}---many directions to
differ along, freedom to move among them, and qualities one can sense in
place.

Two lineages of systems have taken up the lower-right cell. The first builds a
\emph{virtual library}---an interface that brings the physical library's
explorability onto the screen. Pejtersen's Book House furnished the screen as a
house filled with books, and gave each work a texture to sense: icons evoking
what a book holds, so that its feel could be caught without opening
it~\cite{Pejtersen198940,Pejtersen1983230}; its heirs now walk the aisles in
virtual reality~\cite{Lemos201930}. Each work can be felt in place; the space
between works stays unmapped.

The second lineage maps that space. Works are laid out on a plane, similar works
placed nearer, and the seeker wanders freely, touching
works~\cite{Pampalk2002570,Knees2020165}. The procedure is largely shared:
prepare the data the layout needs and hand it to an existing visualization
method. Even mood can be mapped: experts annotate the works with mood words, and
the layout follows~\cite{Oliveira2011105,Andjelkovic2019142}.

In both lineages there is a common pattern: the space for connotative
exploration is structured by hand, typically through domain knowledge made
explicit for the system to carry---often as verbal tags or labels. Generic
content features are not used for this; in them, denotative and connotative
aspects remain mixed. Here our question arises: can a space for connotative
exploration be drawn from the collection data as they arrive---no new names
coined for connotation, and the names already carried serving only to mark what
it is not?

\subsection{Representation Learning and the Disentanglement
    Challenge}\label{subsec:disentanglement}

The question just posed can be restated as a problem about the data.
What we are given is the content data of the works themselves, and it
carries both aspects: the denotative, which descriptors already name, and the connotative, which they do not. That content can carry
what no descriptor does, the scene of
Section~\ref{sec:introduction} has already shown: what stopped the
visitor in the station was sound alone, with no record in sight.
But in the data the two lie mixed, and left as they are, they collapse into a single distance. They
must be pulled apart.

In machine learning, this is precisely the problem that representation learning
names~\cite{Bengio20131798}. A representation in which distinct
factors of variation occupy separable directions is called
\textit{disentangled}~\cite{Higgins2017}; what we seek is a
representation of the content data in which denotation and connotation are
disentangled. In such a space \textit{denotatively
    unexpected and connotatively relevant}
(Section~\ref{subsec:serendipity_process}) acquires
coordinates---denotatively distant, yet connotatively close---and the
space itself, told as a library, is the library with rabbit holes of
Section~\ref{sec:introduction}.

Constructing such a disentangled representation is a recognized open challenge.
In the unsupervised setting it is provably ill-posed: for any disentangled
solution, infinitely many entangled ones generate the same
observations~\cite{Locatello20197247}. Some \textit{inductive bias}---a
structural assumption about the space---is therefore unavoidable; the question
is not whether to adopt one, but which one suits the pairing at hand.
Section~\ref{sec:framework} paints the space; Section~\ref{sec:mathematics}
shows that, once we state what the connotation coordinate should express, an
appropriate bias follows naturally, in the form of optimal transport.
