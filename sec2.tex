\section{Three lineages}\label{sec:lineages}

The opening scene touches three questions at once, and this field has
pursued all three---in three lineages that seldom cite one another. How to
\emph{write down} what the visitor described is a question the tradition of
knowledge organization has asked about representation. What the visitor was
\emph{seeking} is a question the study of information behaviour has asked
about the seeker. How he might have been \emph{brought} to Debussy is a
question retrieval research has asked about the means of access. This
section follows each lineage to its endpoint. Each subsection can be read
independently, and a reader at home in one lineage may check its closing
paragraph and move on; it is the overlay of the three endpoints, in
Section~\ref{subsec:meet}, that carries the argument forward.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsection{The first lineage: representing what has no name}
\label{subsec:first}

This lineage asks about the form of the record: in what form could
``quiet, with feeling, melting colours'' be written down?

Its central instrument has been the name. Facet analysis organizes a
domain by \emph{characteristics of division}: divide recordings by period,
and the resulting classes are mutually exclusive and jointly exhaustive. In
the logician's terms the device is \emph{intensional}---a class is fixed by
stating a property its members must possess---and the confidence in
intension is written into the field's foundations. Dahlberg's concept
theory gives characteristics priority in the constitution of
concepts~\cite{Dahlberg1978142}; the terminological standard ISO~704
explicitly prefers intensional definition and admits extensional
definition---the enumeration of subordinate concepts---only where the
enumeration is closed~\cite{ISO704}. Ranganathan's five fundamental
categories claimed that any subject could be analysed as their
combination; the Classification Research Group found five too few and
moved to thirteen~\cite{Broughton200649}. A question surfaces in that
revision---are fundamental categories universal, or relative to a
domain?---to which we return in Section~\ref{sec:mathematics}.

The visitor said ``impressionistic'', and the word repays attention.
Debussy is routinely classed under \emph{Impressionism}; had the librarian
taken the word as a genre term, she could have reached the answer. But this
lucky near-miss exposes the structure of the problem: the label
\emph{Impressionism} excludes Miles Davis---the very work the visitor
started from. A label that expels the seeker's own point of departure is no
expression of his criterion. Nor was the visitor naming a style; he was
groping. The same string of letters is, in one mouth, a precise stylistic
concept and, in another, a tentative adjective---and what the visitor needs
is the second, which the first can only approximate, with losses.

Why can no facet be constructed for ``quiet, with feeling, melting
colours''? The quality comes in degrees, interferes with other qualities,
and arises to different extents for different listeners: it breaks every
condition of facethood at once. What deserves emphasis is that this limit
was identified \emph{from within} the lineage, and that identifying it was
an achievement, not a failure. Wilson demonstrated that the methods for
determining a document's subject are several and do not agree---even the
supposedly denotative \emph{subject} is indeterminate~\cite{Wilson1968}.
Beghtol brought text linguistics to aboutness and then showed that fiction
resists aboutness analysis~\cite{Beghtol198684,Beghtol1994}: a novel is not
comfortably ``about'' anything, and instrumental music still less. The
lineage reaches its sharpest formulation in Mai: indexing ``is not a
neutral and objective representation of a document's subject matter but
the representation of an interpretation of a document for future
use''~\cite{Mai2001591}. On Mai's Peircean account interpretation is an
unlimited chain of signs, and to assign a label is to halt that chain at a
point of the indexer's choosing.

The field did not abandon connotative attributes upon learning this. It
kept trying, under several names, and the attempts are instructive. Osgood
and colleagues offered the semantic differential explicitly as the
measurement of \emph{connotative} meaning: many bipolar adjective scales,
factor-analysed, yield a small number of recurrent
dimensions~\cite{Osgood1957}. Note the structure of the method: the
individual adjectives are named; the recovered dimensions are not. Humans
rate on named scales, and the identification of dimensions is left to
statistics---a structure that will return in Section~\ref{subsec:third}.
In image description, Shatford recast Panofsky's iconography into a matrix
whose \emph{Abstract} layer is where mood and atmosphere
fall~\cite{Shatford198639}; Shatford Layne judged that this layer, open as
it is to personal interpretation, cannot be indexed ``with any
consistency''---while insisting that subjectivity is no reason to abandon
it~\cite{ShatfordLayne1994583}; Svenonius, in the same year, set out the
limits of subject indexing for visual and aural
languages~\cite{Svenonius1994600}---precisely the territory of our opening
scene. J{\"o}rgensen found that when people describe images, abstract
attributes account for some two per cent of what they
say~\cite{Jorgensen1998161}: people do not speak of atmosphere even when
facing it, and the visitor's groping is not the exception but the norm. In
fiction retrieval, Pejtersen began from recorded user--librarian
conversations and condensed the finding: users of fiction do not ask for
particular books to be identified; their criteria ``are related to a
combination of several different features, such as subject matter,
emotional experience, readability''~\cite{Pejtersen1983230}. Connotative
qualities appear as multidimensional composites---as the visitor's three
adjectives did. On the practical side, appeal factors gave readers'
advisory a working set of dimensions~\cite{Saricks1989}, and Rafferty and
Hidderley, arguing that controlled vocabulary cannot fully index creative
works, proposed indexing by user participation
instead~\cite{Rafferty2005}. One observation from this work should be put
on record: the bipolar sliders descended from appeal factors presuppose
that connotation comes in \emph{degrees}. ``A little quieter'' is a
meaningful operation only if connotation carries a structure of nearness.
Facets deliver discrete classes; connotation demands a gradient.
Section~\ref{sec:mathematics} takes this up.

Across these attempts one phenomenon recurs. Give a connotative attribute
a controlled label, and the label loses the very quality it was meant to
capture. ``Impressionistic'' hardened into \emph{Impressionism} becomes
searchable and consistent---and drops Miles Davis. Widen the label to
``quiet, emotional music'' and both musicians enter, along with a flood of
unrelated recordings. Fine granularity loses members; coarse granularity
admits noise; either way, what the seeker had encircled is gone. No
established term names this phenomenon. Mai's unlimited semiosis supplies
its mechanism; Bowker and Star's residual categories, and Olson's power to
name, each catch a part of it~\cite{Bowker1999,Olson2002}. We will call
it, provisionally, \emph{flattening by labelling}. The phenomenon is
empirically attested. Indexing a hundred works of medieval art without
controlled vocabulary, thirty-nine indexers agreed on terms at an average
of seven per cent, on concepts at thirteen~\cite{Markey1984155}; Saarti
concluded that a novel has no single meaning to be
analysed~\cite{Saarti200249}; among appeal descriptors, the most used are
also the most disagreed upon~\cite{Lin2018869}; and in the annotation of
mood generally, agreement rises as the number of classes falls. There are
two ways to read this pattern. The first: labelling is coarsening, and
coarsening flattens connotative information. The second, darker reading:
\emph{connotative attributes cannot be measured at all.} The question is a
heavy one for this field. We leave it open here;
Section~\ref{sec:framework} answers it.

If intension is closed to us and labels destroy what they touch, one form
remains: \emph{extension}---the set of works that share the quality. In
the opening scene, the set \{the recordings around Miles Davis, Debussy's
piano works, \ldots\} would itself be the expression of the visitor's
criterion. No name is given; the set is shown. And the form has an
established formal counterpart: \emph{polythetic classification}, proposed
in taxonomy by Sneath and carried into anthropology by
Needham~\cite{Sneath1962,Needham1975349}. Its defining feature: no single
attribute is shared by all members. Members share many attributes
pairwise, yet nothing runs through the whole---and still the class
coheres. This is the opening set exactly. Miles Davis and Debussy share no
facet value whatever, and the set coheres: \emph{the opening set is a
polythetic class.} Needham reported the concept's independent convergence
with Wittgenstein's family resemblance~\cite{Wittgenstein1953}; Kuhn
located paradigms in shared exemplars rather than shared
rules~\cite{Kuhn1962}; prototype theory found categories represented by
remembered instances rather than definitions~\cite{Rosch1975573}. But
recall the standard: extensional definition is admitted only as
\emph{closed} enumeration~\cite{ISO704}, and the opening set cannot be
closed. Recordings not yet heard, works not yet written, belong to it.
Extension is the right form---and unusable as it stands.

The endpoint of the first lineage, then. Connotative qualities admit no
expression by name: to label them is to flatten what one reached for. The
one remaining form is extension, formally identified as the polythetic
class; but extensional definition has been confined to closed enumeration,
and how to express an open set is unresolved---as is the darker question
of whether connotative attributes can be measured at all.

\subsection{The second lineage: what the seeker seeks}
\label{subsec:second}

Where the first lineage interrogated the work, this one interrogates the
seeker. What happens at the moment the visitor thinks, \emph{so this is
what I was looking for}?

The word \emph{serendipity} is Walpole's
coinage~\cite{Walpole1754,MertonBarber2004}, but the formulation that
matters here is Merton's. The serendipity pattern, he wrote, ``refers to
the fairly common experience of observing an unanticipated, anomalous and
strategic datum which becomes the occasion for developing a new theory or
for extending an existing theory''~\cite{Merton1948505}. Look closely at
what the unexpected datum occasions: not a new fact about the world, but a
reformulation of the observer's own theory. The surprise comes from
outside; what changes is inside. Merton's serendipity was reflexive at its
origin. The opening scene reproduces the structure exactly---what the
visitor gained was not knowledge of Debussy but knowledge of his own
criterion, which had existed, unverbalized, all along. The same structure
hides in Pasteur's ``chance favours only the prepared
mind''~\cite{Pasteur1854}: the prepared mind is the one that holds, still
unarticulated, the criteria by which it will recognize the encounter when
it comes.

On this ground LIS has built a vigorous research tradition, and the works
below each sharpened the phenomenon's structure. We put to them a single
question: \emph{where has the discovered thing been placed?} The turning
point was Foster and Ford, who moved serendipity from happy accident to a
phenomenon arising from both conditions and strategies, shifting its
weight onto the seeker's preparedness~\cite{Foster2003321}---very close to
our concern; yet what is discovered remains information in the service of
the problem at hand. Erdelez's information encountering is likewise the
unexpected finding of useful or interesting
\emph{information}~\cite{Erdelez1999}. Makri and Blandford, in the
finest-grained analysis of the process, resolved serendipity into
unexpectedness, insight and value~\cite{Makri2012part1,Makri2012part2};
but the insight in question is the forming of a new connection---insight
about the world. Describe the opening scene in this framework and
everything fits: the visitor unexpectedly encountered Debussy, and the
encounter proved valuable. Yet the fact that he learned his own criterion
falls out of the description. That omission is the blind spot of this
lineage. Bj{\"o}rneborn contrasted convergent and divergent behaviour in
the physical library~\cite{Bjorneborn2008}---a contrast to which
Section~\ref{sec:framework} will give a formal reading; McCay-Peet and
Toms separated the quality of the event from the experience of the
process~\cite{McCayPeet20151463}. Closest of all stand Ross, whose study
of readers made meaning something constructed on the reader's
side~\cite{Ross1999783}, and Agarwal, who suggested that serendipity
satisfies Taylor's visceral need~\cite{Agarwal2015}. One step remains in
each---and, so far as we can determine, no study has taken it. None
formulates the discovered thing as the seeker's own unarticulated
criterion. Merton's reflexive structure was not inherited. The
computational register shows the same displacement: serendipity metrics
for recommender systems are formulated as properties of the delivered
items~\cite{Kotkov2016180}, even as that literature itself protests that
serendipitousness lies strictly in the user's mind.

Yet the same field holds a second stream, one that made unnameability its
explicit subject. Taylor, analysing how questions transform in the
reference interview, distinguished four levels of need---visceral,
conscious, formalized, compromised~\cite{Taylor1968178}. The opening scene
is his textbook case: the ``atmosphere I cannot quite name'' is visceral;
the three groped adjectives are the move to conscious; and what the
librarian finally types---``Miles Davis'', ``jazz''---is compromised, the
need pared down at every step. Belkin, Oddy and Brooks's anomalous state
of knowledge put it more strongly: an information need cannot, in
principle, be precisely specified by its owner, and the system should
therefore not demand a specification~\cite{Belkin198261}---by which light
the librarian's ``something more specific, please'' was precisely the
wrong request. Kuhlthau's search process pivots on focus formulation: the
focus is not carried into the search but formed within
it~\cite{Kuhlthau1991361}; in the scene it forms at the moment of Debussy.
Bates's berrypicking has the query, and the understanding of the need
itself, evolving with each document met~\cite{Bates1989407}. Furnas and
colleagues established the vocabulary problem: two people name the same
thing alike with dismayingly low probability~\cite{Furnas1987964}---a
finding that will return, in an unexpected role, in
Section~\ref{sec:framework}. Beyond LIS, behavioural decision research
went further still: preferences are often \emph{constructed} in the
process of elicitation, not revealed by
it~\cite{Slovic1995364,Lichtenstein2006}; Polanyi's ``we can know more
than we can tell'' stands behind the whole stream~\cite{Polanyi1966}.

Now set the two streams side by side---and notice that they hardly cite
each other; Agarwal's remark is the clearest bridge we can
find~\cite{Agarwal2015}. One stream says: the unexpected is found. The
other says: the need cannot be named. They are two faces of one
phenomenon, and their non-intersection is no accident, for the moment they
are joined, a conclusion follows at once. If the need cannot be named in
advance, its satisfaction cannot be a matching---there is no expression to
match against. Satisfaction can only occur as \emph{recognition after the
encounter}. The visitor does not know, until Debussy, precisely what he
was seeking; at the encounter, he knows. In connotative access, then,
serendipity is not a peripheral accident. It is the normal mode in which
criteria are discovered.

The endpoint of the second lineage: the seeker's target cannot be named in
advance and is recognized only after the encounter---serendipity is the
normal mode of connotative access. But the field has consistently placed
the recognized object in the world: information, works, connections. The
reflexive structure of Merton's original---that what is recognized is the
seeker's own frame---was left unclaimed.

\subsection{The third lineage: reaching without naming}
\label{subsec:third}

The first lineage asked about the record, the second about the seeker;
this one asks about the means that connect them. Before following its
history, one distinction must be laid down, for the conclusion of the
whole section will rest on it. A means of access can be built into the
\emph{record}, or into the \emph{dialogue}. The first we call a
\emph{representation}: a subject heading is written into the bibliographic
record, survives its maker, and serves other libraries. The second we call
an \emph{interface}: the dialogue design of a search screen works for the
duration of a session and ends with it.

The traditional mode of access is \emph{predicative}: the user states a
term, and the system returns what was described by it. Cutter's second
object---collocation, the gathering of like with like---is the central
institutional achievement of the catalogue~\cite{Cutter1876}, and it rests
on description being recorded. The premises of predicative access are
equally plain: that the user can say the word, and that the work carries
it. The visitor satisfies neither.

There is another mode: to \emph{show} instead of name. The visitor did it
first, and untaught---``like Miles Davis'' is not a predication but an act
of pointing. Its formal treatment in retrieval is \emph{ostensive access}:
Campbell and van Rijsbergen modelled an information need as a developing
thing, weighting the exemplars a user indicates by their
recency~\cite{Campbell1996251}. The mode sidesteps the first lineage's
trap: ostension adds no label, and so halts no chain of interpretation;
flattening by labelling cannot occur. Pejtersen's Book House belongs here
by its implementation: iconic, nonverbal query input---the first serious
system to let people search without naming~\cite{Pejtersen1983230}. And
the scene shows why exemplars must be several. ``Like Miles Davis'' alone
leaves undecided \emph{which} of that music's dozens of properties is
meant: the trumpet, the modal harmony, the decade, the quiet. Only when
Debussy is added do genre, instrument and period fall away, and the shared
residue narrows.

But ostension carries four limits. First, cold start: the user must
already possess an exemplar. Second, it cannot be recorded: an ostensive
query leaves no trace in the record, nothing that can be handed to
another. Third, it cannot be inspected: the reversibility of explanation
that facets provide---\emph{why} was this retrieved?---is lost. Fourth,
underdetermination: finitely many exemplars never fix the rule that
extends them, and which shared dimension counts is decided not by the user
but by the similarity measure. (Goodman's new riddle of induction and
Wittgenstein's rule-following problem are the philosophical forms of
this~\cite{Goodman1955,Wittgenstein1953}.) For libraries, the second limit
is the decisive one, and the scene shows its weight. Suppose the librarian
had known the Miles--Debussy kinship: she could have answered on the spot.
But where could she have \emph{written that knowledge down}? No field of
the bibliographic record receives it. It would leave when she leaves, and
the next visitor would grope for words from the beginning. An institution
is, precisely, the practice of writing things down so that they can be
handed on; an access mode that cannot be recorded may live as personal
skill, but it does not accumulate. Here is why the connotative quadrant of
Table~\ref{tab:quadrants} stands nearly empty---not conceptual
impossibility, but an infrastructural fact. Bibliographic records describe
items one at a time; ostension demands a structure of similarity
\emph{between} items. The tradition built collocation by author and by
subject; it did not build collocation by connotative likeness. Did
not---which is not the same as could not.

To support ostension computationally, features must be derived from the
works themselves. The story of that effort is often told as ``then machine
learning changed everything''; the record says otherwise---the watershed
lies elsewhere. On one route, the analyst names the dimension and
prescribes its computation. Flesch's readability formula reduced ease of
reading to two surface counts, and the standing criticism is that it
contains no theory of comprehension and no reader~\cite{Flesch1948221}.
Tamura and colleagues approximated six textural qualities by formula,
matching computation to the words people actually use of texture---and
concluded,
themselves, that the discrepancies they met ``indicate fundamental
problems in digital analysis of textures''~\cite{Tamura1978460}. Hevner
mapped mode, tempo, pitch and harmony onto circles of mood adjectives,
admirably explicit about the correspondence---and the vocabulary has aged
out from under the scheme~\cite{Hevner1936246}. The failures share one
mechanism: this route requires dimensions to be named in advance, and
naming is exactly what connotative attributes resist. On the other route,
dimensions are not named but \emph{derived}. Multidimensional scaling
recovers a low-dimensional space from similarity judgments alone; its
dimensions carry no names, and what it recovers is not positions but a
structure of nearness~\cite{Shepard1962125,Kruskal19641}---this field has
been putting nearness into spaces for over half a century. Latent semantic
indexing decomposes the term--document matrix into some hundred factors
that are, in principle, unnamed~\cite{Deerwester1990391}: the same
structure as Osgood's method in Section~\ref{subsec:first}, named scales
in, unnamed dimensions out. A historical question then poses itself. The
vector space model was representing documents as feature vectors, without
machine learning, in 1975~\cite{Salton1975613}. Why did connotative access
not follow? Because the model's dimensions are words, and connotation does
not live in single words; it lives in patterns of their distribution. The
reduction that recovers such patterns is what latent semantic indexing
added in 1990, as a direct response to that limit. The true watershed,
then, is not machine learning against what came before, but dimensions
named by hand against dimensions derived from the data: latent dimensions
precede neural networks by decades, and modern embeddings continue this
route rather than break with it. One premise should also be put on record:
since 1975 the field has accepted that works are mutually comparable, that
distance between works is meaningful. Section~\ref{sec:mathematics} will
lean on exactly this.

The lineage contains one further mechanism, easily passed over. In the
language-modelling approach to retrieval, each document defines its own
generative model~\cite{Ponte1998}; and Lavrenko and Croft showed how, from
a \emph{set} of relevant documents, to estimate the \emph{distribution}
of the relevance class~\cite{Lavrenko2001120}. Note what this operation
is: a set is given; a distribution is derived. The distribution
expresses---without stating it---what the members share, and it is no
closed enumeration, for it passes judgment on objects not yet observed.
Latent topic models share the structure~\cite{Hofmann1999,Blei2003993},
and Chang and colleagues added a telling detail: the better a topic
model's statistical fit, the worse, at times, its human
interpretability~\cite{Chang2009}. A derived intension can be named after
the fact; the naming is not guaranteed. (A parallel stream in
Japan---kansei engineering and impression-based retrieval---assembled a
complete methodology from semantic differentials and multivariate
analysis~\cite{Nagamachi19953,Kato1992112}, with almost no citation across
the language boundary: the same problem, worked twice, independently.)

The newest chapter of this lineage was written mostly outside libraries.
Today the road most travelled to ``something like Miles Davis'' runs
through a streaming service, and its workhorse, collaborative filtering,
has an instructive structure when seen from here. It never looks at the
works. It factorizes the record of who listened to what, and latent
dimensions emerge on which preference is predictable: a stand-in for
connotative proximity, constructed from the behaviour of other users, for
want of access to the content. And it works. But consider the coordinate
system it yields. It is not unique: the factorization is free up to
rotation, so the same data admit infinitely many equivalent systems, and
nothing in the mathematics prefers one. It is relative to its population:
change the users and the coordinates change; the system is an image of how
that crowd happened to behave. It is not a bibliographic object: it cannot
be written into a record and shared across libraries; it lives inside a
provider, shifts with every retraining, and guarantees no reproducibility.
And it compresses each user into a single vector---a decision whose
consequences we draw in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}. Collaborative
filtering thus \emph{delivers} connotative access while lacking every
qualification of a representation: a coordinate system hidden behind an
interface, neither unique nor public.

The endpoint of the third lineage: access has two modes, predication and
ostension. Ostension avoids the label and its flattening, but cannot be
recorded, and so never accumulated institutionally. In deriving features
from works, the route that succeeded derives dimensions rather than naming
them; a mechanism already exists that turns finite sets into open rules of
judgment; and the de facto modern solution provides connotative access
through a coordinate system that is neither unique, nor public, nor
bibliographic. What this lineage has produced are interfaces. What it has
not produced is a representation.

\subsection{Where the lineages meet}\label{subsec:meet}

Now set the three endpoints side by side.

The first lineage requires that the expression of a connotative quality be
extensional, and leaves two defects standing: the enumeration is closed,
and a shown set---like an ostensive query---is nothing one can write down.
The third lineage holds the mechanism that repairs both at a stroke.
Derive a distribution from the set, and the enumeration opens: the
distribution judges recordings not yet heard---does this belong with
those?---expressing what the members share without ever saying it. And a
distribution can be indicated by a \emph{position}. A position can be
written down. At that moment, what was an interface becomes a
representation. The knowledge our librarian had nowhere to put---that
Miles Davis and Debussy share something---acquires a place in the record:
not as a name, but as a position. The empty quadrant fills.

The second lineage then dictates how positions are found. The seeker
cannot state coordinates; to ask for them would be to demand a formalized
need from a visceral one---the demand the ASK argument ruled out, the
librarian's ``something more specific, please''. A position can only be
narrowed the way the visitor narrowed his: through encounter, and
recognition. The second lineage's deepest finding is that this is no
makeshift but the only way.

And at the intersection, what the second lineage had left unclaimed is
recovered. What the visitor recognized in Debussy was not the piece; the
piece was the occasion. What he recognized was that his target \emph{is
expressed as this position}. That is Merton's reflexive structure,
expressible at last in the form the other two lineages supply. The next
section states it as a proposal.
