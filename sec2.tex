%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{Serendipity in Information Systems: Prior Work and Research
  Challenge}\label{sec:serendipity}

Research has long known the scene of Section~\ref{sec:introduction}. It has
studied serendipity, asked what a record can hold, and built systems for the
encounter. This section follows that work to where its reach ends.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsection{What Serendipity Is}\label{subsec:serendipity_process}

Horace Walpole coined \textit{serendipity} after \textit{The Three Princes of
  Serendip}, whose heroes made discoveries by a mixture of accident and
sagacity~\citep{Armeno1557,Walpole1754}. Both have stood in
the word ever since: the chance encounter, and the prepared mind that
\citet{Pasteur1854} said chance favors. Serendipity is therefore chance met
halfway by a mind in motion.

Research keeps both. Chance appears in the encounter: the serendipitous item is
often characterized as \textit{unexpected and relevant}~\citep{Kotkov2016180},
with variations across the field~\citep{Ziarani2021375,Hasan2023933}. Activity,
in turn, appears in the seeker: the likelihood of meeting such an item depends
on disposition and engagement~\citep{Foster2003321,Erdelez199926,Makri20142179}.
Yet the two differ in one respect---control. Activity can be cultivated;
chance, by contrast, cannot be commanded~\citep{McBirnie2008600}.

Research adds a third feature: the encounter changes the seeker. Unexpectedness
arrives mixed with insight---a new way of seeing, a new question, a recognition
of what one had been seeking---as \citet{Makri2012684} found it. Serendipity
is accordingly understood as a \emph{process}: one meets the unexpected yet
sought-for, grasps its significance, and comes away with a valuable
outcome~\citep{McCayPeet20151463,Busch20241110}. The find, then, is
the occasion; what matters takes place in the finder~\citep{Merton1948505}.

The visitor of Section~\ref{sec:introduction} shows all three at once: he was
seeking actively, the encounter with Debussy was unplanned, and it clarified
what he sought. The scene also shows what the definitions leave open---how far
his own activity could reach. Active search begins once a need becomes words
the system will take. The visitor's need stops short of that: a need of this
kind cannot in principle be precisely specified by the one who has
it~\citep{Belkin198261}.

What he could hand the system, then, were names---\textit{Kind of Blue}, jazz,
trumpet, 1959---and a name carries denotation. His activity could therefore reach that far and no
further. Beyond lay connotation, and there chance took over: the street piano he
had not gone to hear. Hence the pairing can now be stated exactly: serendipity
is \textit{denotatively unexpected and connotatively relevant}. Denotation can
be actively explored; connotation, on this account, can only be waited for. This
paper works on that line, asking how far activity can be carried before chance
takes over---not how chance may be commanded. We begin with how the field has
handled denotation and connotation.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsection{Denotation and Connotation}\label{subsec:naming}

Denotation is what a name can hold, and the field built a superb instrument
for it. Bibliographic control is a technology of naming: it describes a work
by author, genre, subject, and other facets. \citet{Cutter1876} set these
aims for the catalogue, and facet analysis now underlies retrieval well
beyond the library~\citep{Broughton200649}. Denotation is therefore what
conventional systems handle best: it can be named, recorded, indexed, and
searched.

Connotation is different. It is the second layer of meaning---what a sign
carries beyond what it plainly denotes~\citep{Rafferty2011283}---and it lives
where a work and a person meet~\citep{Rosenblatt1978}. That meeting place keeps
it beyond the reach of any one record, in two ways. It has no name: what a work
expresses, \citet{Svenonius1994600} argues, has no name and therefore no index
term, and the visitor of Section~\ref{sec:introduction} could offer only
\textit{colors that seem to melt into one another}. It answers to the person: it
differs from one person to the next~\citep{Cosijn2000533}.
\citet{Saracevic1996201} named this a \textit{motivational or affective}
relevance. Even so, this paper takes connotation in the narrow sense a retrieval
system can act on: a quality with no name, yet felt in common across works and
across people. Mood is the case in point. How, then, can a system hold what no
name can?

The field answers in three ways. The first makes denotation finer: mood itself
becomes a descriptor on the
record~\citep{Pejtersen1983230,Shatford198639,Saricks2005,Cho2023567}, and what
a work expresses is pursued until the descriptors have covered it. However, the
finer the descriptors, the harder they are to apply
consistently~\citep{Saarti200249}. More fundamentally, this approach merely
shifts the boundary between denotation and connotation: whatever the
descriptors reach becomes denotation, and connotation begins again on the far
side.

The second way uses non-exclusive labels in place of exclusive descriptors, and
represents connotation as a spectrum of those labels. Factor analysis gives
labels to the axes~\citep{Osgood1957}; correspondence analysis sets the labels
in the space itself~\citep{Andjelkovic2019142}. A work may then lie between the
labels, not under one of them. Still, the labels span the space, and no
connotation reaches beyond it.

The third way sets words aside and works from the content data: generic
features place each work~\citep{Pampalk2002570,Knees2020165}. This seems to
leave words behind altogether. But the tie is cut both ways: nothing then
tells connotation from denotation. The widest differences are denotative, so
genre returns as geography. What this leaves for a seeker who goes looking is
the question of the next section.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsection{Serendipity in Information Systems}\label{subsec:systems}

A seeker may act in two ways, and a third acts for him. \textit{Search} asks
him to say what he seeks: \citet{Taylor1968178} traced the path from a need
merely felt to a question finally asked, and the system waits at the end of
it. Where the visitor's names run out, search runs out with them.
\textit{Exploration} asks nothing of the kind: it hands him a structured
space and lets him move. \textit{Recommendation} is the third, where works
are given rather than sought; its serendipity literature is
rich~\citep{Kotkov2016180,Ziarani2021375}, and we set it aside here. What
follows asks how exploration has served connotation.

Exploration gives terrain instead of a query: the user moves, and the system
provides the ground~\citep{Marchionini200641}.
The seeking itself moves with what it finds~\citep{Bates1989407}, and
serendipity asks that much of the seeker: he must be in motion. The physical
library has always excelled here. \citet{Bjorneborn20171053} named the
affordances that make it so: \emph{diversifiability},
\emph{traversability}, and \emph{sensoriability}---many directions to differ
along, freedom to move among them, and qualities one can sense in place.

Research has focused on two layers. The first is the interface, where the
visitor experiences exploration. The line runs from Pejtersen's Book
House~\citep{Pejtersen198940} to the virtual reality of
today~\citep{Lemos201930}.

The second layer is the exploration space itself. Here the second and third
ways of Section~\ref{subsec:naming} return: the space has been built from
labels~\citep{Oliveira2011105,Andjelkovic2019142}, and from generic
features~\citep{Pampalk2002570,Knees2020165}. It inherits the limit of
each. Once the space is obtained, the interfaces of the first layer can be
built on it.

\paragraph{Core question.}
Every road forks at naming, and neither branch arrives. Keep the names, and
nothing lies past them. Drop them, and nothing tells the named from the
unnamed. Naming is the foundation: without it, nothing can be built. Yet a
foundation alone raises nothing into connotative space. Here, then, is our
question: can the descriptors lay that foundation, and the content data itself
raise the building---a multi-dimensional library space in which both the
denotative and the connotative can be explored?
