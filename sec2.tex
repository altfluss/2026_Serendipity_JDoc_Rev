\section{Serendipity in Information Systems: Prior Work and Research
  Challenge}\label{sec:serendipity}

The scene of Section~\ref{sec:introduction} is not new to research. This
section reviews the work it touches---on serendipity, on what a record can
hold, on systems built for encounters---with one aim: to make clear,
standing on that work, what the next step forward requires, and to give
that requirement its technical name.


%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsection{What Serendipity Is}\label{subsec:serendipity_process}

The word \textit{serendipity} was coined by Horace Walpole after
\textit{The Three Princes of Serendip}, whose heroes made their
discoveries by a mixture of accident and
sagacity~\cite{The-Three-Princes-of-Serendip,Walpole1754}. From the start
the notion held two things together: a chance encounter, and the prepared
mind which, in Pasteur's phrase, chance
favors~\cite{Pasteur1854}. Serendipity is not luck befalling a passive
observer; it is met halfway by a mind in motion.

Research has restated this pairing in many forms: the serendipitous item
is at once \textit{unexpected and relevant}~\cite{Kotkov2016180}, with
variations across the
field~\cite{Ziarani2021375,Chen2019,Hasan2023933,Nahm2023}, and the
likelihood of meeting one turns on the seeker's own disposition and active
engagement~\cite{Foster2003321,Erdelez199926,Makri20142179}. Of the two
halves, only the active one answers to the seeker. McBirnie, asking
whether serendipity can be sought at all, found that it cannot be
commanded, though what the seeker brings to it can be
cultivated~\cite{McBirnie2008600}.

To this pairing the literature adds a third constant: the encounter
transforms. What is gained is never only a pleasing find; unexpectedness
arrives mixed with insight, as Makri and Blandford heard
it~\cite{Makri2012684}---a new way of seeing, a new question, a
recognition of what one had been seeking. Serendipity is accordingly
understood as a \emph{process}: one explores, meets the unexpected yet
sought-for, grasps its significance, and comes away with a valuable
outcome~\cite{McCayPeet20151463,Rubin2011,Busch20241110,Corneli2020,Makri2012706}.
The find itself is only the occasion, in Merton's term, for what takes
place in the finder~\cite{Merton1948505}.

The visitor of Section~\ref{sec:introduction} is these findings gathered
in one person. He was active throughout: he asked, he searched, he kept
walking; and his find was \textit{unexpected and relevant} exactly---unexpected
in every attribute the catalogue holds, relevant in a register it does
not. What the encounter gave him was the insight the
process accounts describe: his own criterion.

But the scene shows one thing the definitions do not say: how far his
activity could reach. Active search begins where a need has been brought
into the system's language---Taylor's \emph{compromised}
need~\cite{Taylor1968178}---and a need of this kind is precisely what its
owner cannot state~\cite{Belkin198261}. His search could therefore run on
denotation and only there; where denotation ended, what remained was
chance---the street piano he had not gone to hear. Denotation can be
actively explored. Connotation, it seems, can only be waited for.
Whether that is a fact about connotation or only a fact about our systems
is the question of this paper---and it begins with the bibliographic
record: can connotation be written into one at all?


%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsection{Denotation, Connotation, and What a Record Can
    Hold}\label{subsec:naming}

For denotation, at least, the record is a superb instrument. Bibliographic
control is a technology of naming, and the cataloguing tradition brought
naming to a high finish---from the aims Cutter set for the
catalogue~\cite{Cutter1876} to the facet analysis that now underlies
retrieval well beyond the library~\cite{Broughton200649}. The instrument
serves in the library of Section~\ref{sec:introduction} as well: the
floors are its work, and so is the arrangement of works along their
shelves. Denotation is not the difficulty. It is necessary, and it is well
made.

But not everything a work is can be written into its record. That was our
visitor's whole difficulty---and researchers in several traditions have
long since found it, described it, and shown that it is what users are
after. Shatford, asking what a picture is \textit{of} and what it is
\textit{about}, made the two aspects of one level rather than one beneath
the other: a picture may be about something while being of nothing at all,
and her table carries a band of \textit{non-facet-specific aboutness} that
runs through all four of her facets and answers to no one of
them~\cite{Shatford198639}. The relevance literature arrived at the same
figure: Saracevic set a \textit{motivational or affective} relevance
beside the topical, a relation not of aboutness but of the seeker's own
intents and goals~\cite{Saracevic1996201}; and Cosijn and Ingwersen,
revisiting his scheme, declined to make it a category at all---in their
table it is neither a manifestation of relevance nor an attribute of one,
but a dimension bearing upon them all~\cite{Cosijn2000533}. Each tradition
placed what it found not deeper within the names but across them: not a
column, but a band across the table.

Richer naming does not close the gap, for the limit is structural rather
than lexical. The tradition tried, honestly: Shatford weighed the risk
that codifying moods would narrow the responses a picture is free to
provoke, and chose to index them all the same~\cite{Shatford198639};
Svenonius, pressing the same question further, found that what a work
expresses is not reachable by index terms at
all~\cite{Svenonius1994600}. Mai showed why: an index term is not a
neutral representation of a document's subject but the representation of
an interpretation, a halt imposed on an open chain of
signs~\cite{Mai2001591}; sharpen a label and members fall out, widen it
and noise floods in. Our visitor's set makes the same point from the
user's side: the recordings around Miles Davis, Debussy's piano works,
share no facet value at all, yet cohere---a \textit{polythetic
    class}~\cite{Needham1975349}, and an open one, since works not yet
heard belong to it.

What remains is an asymmetry that shapes everything downstream.
\textbf{Denotation arrives with the record}---it is \emph{descriptor
    data}, given. \textbf{Connotation is written in no field}: if it is to
enter an information system at all, it must be drawn from the
\emph{content data}---the work itself, the track or the text. And it must
enter as the traditions found it---not as a finer name, but laid across
the names, answering to none of them. How remains open: a user-centred
mood vocabulary for fiction, published in these pages in 2023, had to set
aside \textit{interesting} and \textit{absorbing} as too personal to apply
consistently, and mood description, its authors note, remains outside most
cataloguing practice~\cite{Cho2023567}.

\subsection{Serendipity in Information Systems}\label{subsec:systems}

Where does the connotative seeker stand in today's systems?
Table~\ref{tab:quadrants} gives the map: the two kinds of
attribute, crossed with three modes of encounter---searching for what one
can specify, receiving what a system selects, and exploring on one's own.

\begin{table}[t]
    \centering\small
    \caption{Two kinds of attribute crossed with three modes of encounter.}
    \label{tab:quadrants}
    \begin{tabular}{l|ll|l}
        \toprule

        System type & Search                & Exploration        & Recommendation                      \\
        User agency & active                & active             & passive                             \\
        \midrule
        Denotative  & bibliographic search, & faced navigation   & content-based filtering             \\
                    & OPAC                  &                    &                                     \\
        \midrule
        Connotative & (nearly empty)        & reader's advisory, & collaborative filtering,            \\
                    &                       & Book House         & serendipity-oriented recommendation \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

The left column the visitor has already exhausted. Recommendation-based
serendipity research occupies the middle one: the system models the user
and selects, computationally, the items worth surprising them
with~\cite{Kotkov2016180,Ziarani2021375,Shrivastava20231,Kunaver2017,Hasan2023933,Chen2019}.
Collaborative filtering even reaches the connotative row: its latent
factors are proxies for unnamed affinity, derived from the listening of
other users---a fertile path, whose limits are instructive. What it
delivers is an item, selected \emph{for} the user rather than found
\emph{by} them; and the coordinates it derives are free up to rotation---the same data
admit infinitely many equivalent systems---shift as the user population
shifts, and can be written into no bibliographic record.

\textbf{Exploration-based} approaches instead give the user a structured
space to navigate, and the agency reverses: the user moves, and the system
provides the
terrain~\cite{McCayPeet20181,Marchionini200641,Thudt20121461,Beale2007ambient}.
This is where our visitor belongs---serendipity, we saw, is insight met by
a mind in motion---and this is the column in which the physical library
has always excelled. Bj\"orneborn, studying serendipity in physical
libraries, named the affordances that make it so:
\emph{diversifiability}, \emph{traversability}, and
\emph{sensoriability}~\cite{Bjorneborn20171053}---many directions to
differ along, freedom to move among them, and qualities one can sense in
place. The bottom-right cell holds the practices closest to our aim,
readers' advisory and Pejtersen's icon-based Book
House~\cite{Pejtersen1983230}; yet both live at the interface---in the
conversation, the session, the librarian's skill. What the connotative row
still lacks is what Section~\ref{sec:introduction} argued must come first:
a representation---a space beneath any interface whose directions afford
for connotation what Bj\"orneborn's library affords in its aisles.

\subsection{Representation Learning and the Disentanglement
    Challenge}\label{subsec:disentanglement}

The requirement can now be stated in the vocabulary of
\textit{representation learning}~\cite{Bengio20131798}: mapping
observations---the content data---into a space whose distances,
neighborhoods, and paths carry meaning~\cite{Bronstein201718}. What
serendipity research states as \textit{unexpected and
relevant}---denotatively unexpected, connotatively relevant
(Section~\ref{subsec:serendipity_process})---representation learning
states as a
\textit{disentangled representation}~\cite{Higgins2017,Higgins2018}: one
in which the underlying factors of variation align with separable
directions of the space. In our setting the factors are the two kinds of
attribute themselves: one direction is \emph{denotation-fixed}, moving
through connotation while the genre holds; the other is
\emph{connotation-fixed}, crossing genres while the connotation holds.
When the two are separable, a work can be far in one and near in the other
at once. When they are entangled, the two collapse into a single distance,
and the closeness between Davis and Debussy is absorbed into their
categorical separation.

Constructing such a representation is a recognized open challenge. In the
unsupervised setting it is provably ill-posed: for any disentangled
solution, infinitely many entangled ones generate the same
observations~\cite{Locatello20197247}. Some \textit{inductive bias}---a
structural assumption about the space---is therefore unavoidable; the
question is not whether to adopt one, but which one suits the pairing at
hand: denotatively unexpected, connotatively relevant. That is where the present work places itself. The
connotation-fixed direction is precisely the rabbit hole of
Section~\ref{sec:introduction}---among infinitely many candidate
directions, the one that carries meaning.
Section~\ref{sec:framework} paints the space that holds such holes;
Section~\ref{sec:mathematics} shows that, once the scope of the data is
fixed, an appropriate bias follows naturally, in the form of optimal
transport.
