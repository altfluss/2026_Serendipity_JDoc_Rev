\section{Serendipity in Information Systems: Prior Work and Research
  Challenge}\label{sec:serendipity}

The scene of Section~\ref{sec:introduction} is not new to research. This
section reviews the work it touches---on serendipity, on what a record can
hold, on systems built for encounters---with one aim: to locate what a
library with rabbit holes requires that prior work has not yet
supplied, and to give that requirement its technical name.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsection{What Serendipity Is}\label{subsec:serendipity_process}

The word \textit{serendipity} was coined by Horace Walpole after
\textit{The Three Princes of Serendip}, whose heroes made discoveries ``by
accidents and sagacity''~\cite{The-Three-Princes-of-Serendip,Walpole1754}.
From the start the notion held two things together: a chance encounter,
and the prepared mind that recognizes its significance---Pasteur's
``chance favors the prepared mind''~\cite{Pasteur1854}. Serendipity is not
luck befalling a passive observer; it is met halfway by a mind in motion.

Modern research keeps this double structure. Serendipitous encounters are
``unexpected and relevant''~\cite{Kotkov2016180}, a pairing that recurs in
varied wordings across the
field~\cite{Ziarani2021375,Chen2019,Hasan2023933}; the experience itself
has been resolved into ``a mix of unexpectedness and insight'' leading to
a valuable, unanticipated
outcome~\cite{Makri2012part1,Makri2012part2}. That serendipity unfolds as
a \emph{process}---an encounter noticed, judged worth pursuing, and
followed until it reshapes how one sees a
domain~\cite{McCayPeet20151463,Rubin2011,Busch20241110,Corneli2020}---and
that the process runs on the seeker's own active
exploration~\cite{Dervin1983,Makri20142179,Lee20154851} are, by now,
premises the field shares; we take them as given.

What exactly is gained in the encounter? Merton's classical formulation
repays close reading: the serendipity pattern is the experience of
``observing an unanticipated, anomalous and strategic datum which becomes
the occasion for developing a new theory''~\cite{Merton1948505}. The
unexpected datum is the \emph{occasion}; what changes is the observer's
own theory. The surprise comes from outside; the discovery happens inside.
Our visitor reproduces this structure exactly: what he found was not a
piano piece but his own criterion---insight, in Makri and Blandford's
word, here into his own seeking. The information-need literature explains
why such insight cannot be supplied in advance: a need of this kind is
visceral before it is verbalized~\cite{Taylor1968178} and cannot, in
principle, be specified by its owner~\cite{Belkin198261}. It is recognized
at the encounter, not stated before it.

The pair of Section~\ref{sec:introduction} lets these definitional
pairings be read at a glance: each crosses a denotative predicate with a
connotative one. A serendipitous encounter joins works explicitly distant
in denotation and implicitly close in some connotation---\textit{distant
    yet close}, a relation to be treated not as a puzzle but as a structure
awaiting definite form.

\subsection{Denotation, Connotation, and What a Record Can
    Hold}\label{subsec:naming}

\paragraph{The reach of naming.}
Bibliographic control is a technology of naming, and Wilson identified the
two powers at which it can aim: \emph{descriptive} power, to find
everything that bears on a topic, and \emph{exploitative} power, to put
what is found to one's own best use~\cite{Wilson1968}. What the
cataloguing tradition brought to a high finish---from Cutter's
objects~\cite{Cutter1876} through facet analysis~\cite{Broughton200649}---is
the former. Facets implement denotative attributes almost by definition:
sets of mutually exclusive labels, each carried whole by a single word.
For most of what a catalogue does, the instrument serves.

\paragraph{The denotative and the connotative.}
The other kind of meaning has been recognized under many vocabularies.
Semiotics gave the pair its name: denotation and
connotation~\cite{Barthes1977}. Image indexing met it as Panofsky's
iconological layer---mood, symbol, atmosphere---which Shatford's
\emph{of/about} matrix carried into cataloguing
theory~\cite{Shatford198639}; which Shatford Layne judged impossible to
index ``with any consistency,'' while insisting it should not therefore be
abandoned~\cite{ShatfordLayne1994583}; and which Rafferty later mapped
explicitly onto Barthes's pair~\cite{Rafferty2011283}. Svenonius drew
the same limit for visual and aural media
generally~\cite{Svenonius1994600}. Relevance theory met it as the gap
between the \emph{descriptive} relevance a system computes and the
\emph{affective} relevance a user
seeks~\cite{Saracevic1996201,Cosijn2000533,Xu2007179}. Fiction retrieval
met it as appeal, the qualities readers actually ask
for~\cite{Pejtersen1983230}; and on such qualities, trained indexers agree
with one another at rates as low as seven per
cent~\cite{Markey1984155}. The divide is old, robust, and known
under many names. What has been missing is not awareness of the
connotative side, but a form in which it can be held.

\paragraph{Why naming cannot close the gap.}
The limit is structural, not lexical. Indexing, Mai showed, is not a
neutral representation of a document's subject but the representation of
an interpretation---a halt imposed on an open chain of
signs~\cite{Mai2001591}; sharpen a label and members fall out, widen it
and noise floods in. The set our visitor circumscribed---the recordings
around Miles Davis, Debussy's piano works---shares no facet value at all,
yet coheres: a \emph{polythetic class}~\cite{Needham1975349}, and an open
one, since works not yet heard belong to it. No enrichment of vocabulary
closes this gap, for naming is what these qualities resist. Hence an
asymmetry that shapes everything downstream: \textbf{denotation arrives
    with the record}---it is \emph{descriptor data}, given---whereas
\textbf{connotation is written in no field}. If it is to enter an
information system at all, it must be drawn from the \emph{body data}: the
work itself, the track or the text, dense and whole.

\subsection{Serendipity in Information Systems}\label{subsec:systems}

Where does the connotative seeker stand in today's systems?
Table~\ref{tab:quadrants} lays out the terrain: the two kinds of
attribute, crossed with three modes of encounter---searching for what one
can specify, receiving what a system selects, and exploring on one's own.

    \caption{Two kinds of attribute crossed with three modes of encounter.}

        \toprule
                    & Search                     & Recommendation              & Exploration                   \\
        \midrule
        Denotative  & bibliographic search, OPAC & content-based filtering     & faceted navigation            \\
        \midrule
        Connotative & (nearly empty)             & collaborative filtering,    & readers' advisory, Book House \\
                    &                            & serendipity-oriented recsys &                               \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}

Recommendation-based serendipity research occupies the middle column: the
system models the user and selects, computationally, the items worth
surprising them
with~\cite{Kotkov2016180,Ziarani2021375,Shrivastava20231,Kunaver2017,Hasan2023933,Chen2019}.
Collaborative filtering even reaches the connotative row: its latent
factors are proxies for unnamed affinity, derived from the listening of
other users---a fertile path, whose limits are instructive. What it
delivers is an item, selected \emph{for} the user rather than found
\emph{by} them; and the coordinates it derives are free up to rotation,
shift as the user population shifts, and can be written into no
bibliographic record. \textbf{Exploration-based} approaches reverse the
agency: the user moves, and the system provides the
terrain~\cite{McCayPeet20181,Marchionini200641,Thudt20121461,Beale2007ambient}.
This is where our visitor belongs---serendipity, we saw, is insight met by
a mind in motion---and this is the column in which the physical library
has always excelled. Bj\"orneborn, studying serendipity in physical
libraries, named the affordances that make it so:
\emph{diversifiability}, \emph{traversability}, and
\emph{sensoriability}~\cite{Bjorneborn20171053}---many directions to
differ along, freedom to move among them, and qualities one can sense in
place. The bottom-right cell holds the practices closest to our aim,
readers' advisory and Pejtersen's icon-based Book
House~\cite{Pejtersen1983230}; yet both live at the interface---in the
conversation, the session, the librarian's skill. What the connotative row
still lacks is what Section~\ref{sec:introduction} argued must come first:
a representation---a space beneath any interface whose directions afford
for connotation what Bj\"orneborn's library affords in its aisles.

\subsection{Representation Learning and the Disentanglement
    Challenge}\label{subsec:disentanglement}

The requirement can now be stated in the vocabulary of
\textit{representation learning}~\cite{Bengio20131798}: mapping
observations---the body data---into a space whose distances,
neighborhoods, and paths carry meaning~\cite{Bronstein201718}. What
Section~\ref{subsec:serendipity_process} described as a felt relation,
\textit{distant yet close}, representation learning describes as a
\textit{disentangled representation}~\cite{Higgins2017,Higgins2018}: one
in which the underlying factors of variation align with separable
directions of the space. In our setting the factors are the two kinds of
attribute themselves: one direction is \emph{denotation-fixed}, moving
through connotation while the genre holds; the other is
\emph{connotation-fixed}, crossing genres while the connotation holds.
When the two are separable, a work can be far in one and near in the other
at once. When they are entangled, the two collapse into a single distance,
and the closeness between Davis and Debussy is absorbed into their
categorical separation.

Constructing such a representation is a recognized open challenge. In the
unsupervised setting it is provably ill-posed: for any disentangled
solution, infinitely many entangled ones generate the same
observations~\cite{Locatello20197247}. Some \textit{inductive bias}---a
structural assumption about the space---is therefore unavoidable; the
question is not whether to adopt one, but which one is appropriate for
distant yet close. That is where the present work places itself. The
connotation-fixed direction is precisely the rabbit hole of
Section~\ref{sec:introduction}---among infinitely many candidate
directions, the one that carries meaning.
Section~\ref{sec:framework} paints the space that holds such holes;
Section~\ref{sec:mathematics} shows that, once the scope of the data is
fixed, an appropriate bias follows naturally, in the form of optimal
transport.
