\section{Serendipity in Information Systems: Prior Work and Research
  Challenge}\label{sec:serendipity}

The scene of Section~\ref{sec:introduction} is not new to research. This
section reviews the work it touches---on serendipity, on what a record can
hold, on systems built for encounters---with one aim: to make clear,
standing on that work, what the next step forward requires, and to give
that requirement its technical name.


%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsection{What Serendipity Is}\label{subsec:serendipity_process}

The word \textit{serendipity} was coined by Horace Walpole after
\textit{The Three Princes of Serendip}, whose heroes made their
discoveries by a mixture of accident and
sagacity~\cite{The-Three-Princes-of-Serendip,Walpole1754}. From the start
the notion held two things together: a chance encounter, and the prepared
mind which, in Pasteur's phrase, chance
favors~\cite{Pasteur1854}. Serendipity is not luck befalling a passive
observer; it is met halfway by a mind in motion.

Research has restated this pairing in many forms: the serendipitous item
is at once \textit{unexpected and relevant}~\cite{Kotkov2016180}, with
variations across the
field~\cite{Ziarani2021375,Chen2019,Hasan2023933,Nahm2023}, and the
likelihood of meeting one turns on the seeker's own disposition and active
engagement~\cite{Foster2003321,Erdelez199926,Makri20142179}. Of the two
halves, only the active one answers to the seeker. McBirnie, asking
whether serendipity can be sought at all, found that it cannot be
commanded, though what the seeker brings to it can be
cultivated~\cite{McBirnie2008600}.

To this pairing the literature adds a third constant: the encounter
transforms. What is gained is never only a pleasing find; unexpectedness
arrives mixed with insight, as Makri and Blandford heard
it~\cite{Makri2012684}---a new way of seeing, a new question, a
recognition of what one had been seeking. Serendipity is accordingly
understood as a \emph{process}: one explores, meets the unexpected yet
sought-for, grasps its significance, and comes away with a valuable
outcome~\cite{McCayPeet20151463,Rubin2011,Busch20241110,Makri2012706}.
The find itself is only the occasion, in Merton's term, for what takes
place in the finder~\cite{Merton1948505}.

The visitor of Section~\ref{sec:introduction} is these findings gathered
in one person; the scene also shows what the definitions do not: how far
his own activity could reach. Active search can begin only once a need has
been put into words the system will take; Taylor traced that path, from a
need merely felt to a question finally asked~\cite{Taylor1968178}. The
visitor's need never travelled it: such a need cannot, in principle, be
specified by the one who has it~\cite{Belkin198261}. His search could therefore run on denotation,
and only there; where denotation ended, what remained was chance---the
street piano he had not gone to hear. The field's pairing thus takes
an exact form: serendipity is \textit{denotatively unexpected and
    connotatively relevant}.
Denotation can be actively explored.
Connotation, it seems, can only be waited for. But could a seeker's own
exploration reach one step further, into the connotative? That is the
question of this paper; before taking it up, let us see how the field has
handled denotation and connotation.


%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsection{Denotation and Connotation}\label{subsec:naming}

Denotation is the side of a work that names can hold, and for it the
field long ago built a superb instrument. Bibliographic control is a
technology of naming, and the cataloguing tradition brought naming to a
high finish---from the aims Cutter set for the
catalogue~\cite{Cutter1876} to the facet analysis that now underlies
retrieval well beyond the library~\cite{Broughton200649}. The instrument
serves in the library of Section~\ref{sec:introduction} as well: the
floors are its work, and so is the arrangement of works along their
shelves.

Connotation is the second layer of meaning: beneath what a sign plainly
denotes lie the cultural meanings that attach to
it~\cite{Rafferty2011283}, and they arise in the encounter, where a
work and a person meet~\cite{Rosenblatt1978}. Meaning of this kind is
not easy for an information system to handle. First, no invariant word
stands for it. The visitor of Section~\ref{sec:introduction} could
offer only \textit{colors that seem to melt into one another}; indexing
meets the same absence: what a work expresses, Svenonius found, has no
name and therefore no index term~\cite{Svenonius1994600}. Second, only
the one who meets the work can judge it. Saracevic set a
\textit{motivational or affective} relevance beside the topical, a
relation of the seeker's own intents and
goals~\cite{Saracevic1996201}; for Cosijn and Ingwersen it runs
through every subjective judgment, and it excludes the assessor, whose
intentions are not the user's~\cite{Cosijn2000533}. Third, it does not
hold still. The work that fits today's mood is another work
tomorrow~\cite{Ross1999783}, and the seeking itself moves with what it
finds~\cite{Bates1989407}: meeting Debussy deepened what the visitor
had been seeking all along. Yet retrieval lives on what is shared: a meaning wholly one
person's, in one moment, gives a system no purchase; to answer him at
all, some part of connotation must be held in common---across
persons, across moments, and, above all, across works that denotation
keeps apart. Fourth, such commonality stands only on shared ground:
the librarian of Section~\ref{sec:introduction} could feel, at least
in part, what the visitor sought, because both stand on a common
background of language, culture, and embodied
experience~\cite{Clark1996,Langacker2008,Hjorland1995400}; a field in a
record stands on none---it must mean by the symbol alone.

The field has answered along three lines. The first line refines the
labels---Shatford's moods~\cite{Shatford198639}, readers' advisory's
vocabulary of appeal~\cite{Saricks1989}, a recent categorization of
readers' own mood terms~\cite{Cho2023567}---and it
produced richer description, not shared description: when
professionals and patrons indexed the same novels, Saarti found, they
scarcely agreed~\cite{Saarti200249}. The second line does without
words: Osgood's semantic differential recovered unnamed dimensions
from named scales---three of them, everyone's and no work's
own~\cite{Osgood1957}. The third line trusts what users feel:
democratic indexing and social tagging multiplied
voices~\cite{Rafferty2005,Rafferty2011283}---voices that do not add up
to a shared structure. Richer names, fewer names, more voices: each, in its own way, met the
fourth difficulty---a field must mean bare.

How, then, have information systems answered the seeker who comes
looking for the connotative? That is the question of the next
section.

\subsection{Serendipity in Information Systems}\label{subsec:systems}

Table~\ref{tab:quadrants} gives the field's answer in outline: the
two kinds of attribute, crossed with the two active modes of
encounter---searching for what one can specify, and exploring on
one's own. The left column the visitor has already exhausted: its
lower cell is nearly empty, and where denotation ends, search ends
with it.

    \label{tab:quadrants}

        \toprule
                    & Search                     & Exploration                        \\
        \midrule
        Denotative  & bibliographic search, OPAC & faceted navigation                 \\

                    &                            & mood maps                          \\

The right column is where our visitor belongs. Exploration-based
approaches give the user a structured space to navigate: the user
moves, and the system provides the
terrain~\cite{McCayPeet20181,Marchionini200641,Thudt20121461,Beale2007ambient}---and
serendipity, we saw, is insight met by a mind in motion. It is the
column in which the physical library has always excelled. Bj\"orneborn, studying serendipity in physical
libraries, named the affordances that make it so:
\emph{diversifiability}, \emph{traversability}, and
\emph{sensoriability}~\cite{Bjorneborn20171053}---many directions to
differ along, freedom to move among them, and qualities one can sense in
place.

Two lineages of systems have taken up the column. The first builds a
\emph{virtual library}. Pejtersen's Book House furnished the screen
as a house filled with books, and gave each work an artificial
texture to sense: icons evoking what it holds, its feel skimmed
without opening it~\cite{Pejtersen198940,Pejtersen1983230}; its heirs
now walk the aisles in virtual reality~\cite{Lemos201930}. Each work
can be felt in place; the space between works stays unmapped.

The second lineage draws the map. Works are laid out on a plane,
kinship readable as distance, the seeker free to
wander~\cite{Pampalk2002570,Knees2020165}; even mood can be charted so---the works
annotated with mood words by experts, the vectors handed to an
existing visualization
method~\cite{Oliveira2011105,Andjelkovic2019142}. The recipe, so far,
never varies: a denotation made for the purpose, and a layout that
already existed.

Beside searching and exploring stands a third mode of encounter,
recommendation, and it stays off the map by design. There the system
models the user and selects, computationally,
the items worth surprising them
with~\cite{Kotkov2016180,Ziarani2021375,Shrivastava20231,Kunaver2017,Hasan2023933,Chen2019}---a
rich line of research, set aside for one reason: the works arrive, and
our seeker moves. One of its instruments, collaborative filtering,
reaches connotation through no descriptor at all, deriving unnamed
affinity from the listening of many users; what that implies for the
record, we weigh in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

What remains, then, is no longer a question of interface. It is the
question Section~\ref{sec:introduction} argued must come first: the
representation---what the space is made of, before anything is laid
out on a screen.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsection{Representation Learning and the Disentanglement
    Challenge}\label{subsec:disentanglement}

What the seeker needs has been taking shape over the last three
subsections. What he seeks is connotative, and no denotative term
will hold it; so the space that serves him must be one he can explore
by moving. And were the works around him arranged by connotative
nearness, he could seek what he cannot name---feeling the works as he
goes, walking toward what resonates.

The problem is now plain: to arrange works by connotative
kinship. The kinship, moreover, must hold across denotative
difference---otherwise no leap could carry the seeker from one work
to its kin in another genre. And what we are given is the content
data, where connotative information lives; but it lives there
entangled with the denotative, and left as they are, the two collapse
into a single distance. They must be pulled apart.

In machine learning, this is precisely the problem that representation learning
names~\cite{Bengio20131798}. A representation in which distinct
factors of variation occupy separable directions is called
\textit{disentangled}~\cite{Higgins2017}; what we seek is a
representation of the content data in which denotation and connotation are
disentangled. In such a space \textit{denotatively
    unexpected and connotatively relevant}
(Section~\ref{subsec:serendipity_process}) acquires
coordinates---denotatively distant, yet connotatively close---and the
space itself, told as a library, is the library with rabbit holes of
Section~\ref{sec:introduction}.

Constructing such a disentangled representation
is a recognized open challenge. In the unsupervised setting it is provably ill-posed: for any disentangled
solution, infinitely many entangled ones generate the same
observations~\cite{Locatello20197247}. Some \textit{inductive bias}---a
structural assumption about the space---is therefore unavoidable; the question
is not whether to adopt one, but which one suits the pairing at hand.
Section~\ref{sec:framework} paints the space; Section~\ref{sec:mathematics}
shows that, once the scope of the data is fixed, an appropriate bias follows
naturally, in the form of optimal transport.
