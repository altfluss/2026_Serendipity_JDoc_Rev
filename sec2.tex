\section{Serendipity in Information Systems: Prior Work and Research
  Challenge}\label{sec:serendipity}

The scene of Section~\ref{sec:introduction} is not new to research. This
section reviews the work it touches---on serendipity, on what a record can
hold, on systems built for encounters---with one aim: to make clear,
standing on that work, what the next step forward requires, and to give
that requirement its technical name.


%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsection{What Serendipity Is}\label{subsec:serendipity_process}

The word \textit{serendipity} was coined by Horace Walpole after
\textit{The Three Princes of Serendip}, whose heroes made their
discoveries by a mixture of accident and
sagacity~\cite{The-Three-Princes-of-Serendip,Walpole1754}. The notion
has held two elements together from the start: a chance encounter, and
the prepared mind which, in Pasteur's phrase, chance
favors~\cite{Pasteur1854}. Serendipity is not luck befalling a passive
observer; it is met halfway by a mind in motion.

Research has preserved this two-part structure. First, the encounter
contains chance: the serendipitous item is \textit{unexpected and
    relevant}~\cite{Kotkov2016180}, with variations across the
field~\cite{Ziarani2021375,Chen2019,Hasan2023933,Nahm2023}. Second, the
encounter requires activity: the likelihood of meeting such an item
depends on the seeker's disposition and
engagement~\cite{Foster2003321,Erdelez199926,Makri20142179}. The two are
not equally under the seeker's control. Activity can be cultivated;
chance cannot be commanded. McBirnie, asking whether serendipity can be
sought at all, drew the same line~\cite{McBirnie2008600}.

Research identifies a third recurring feature: the encounter changes the
seeker. What is gained is never only a pleasing find; unexpectedness
arrives mixed with insight, as Makri and Blandford heard
it~\cite{Makri2012684}---a new way of seeing, a new question, a
recognition of what one had been seeking. Serendipity is accordingly
understood as a \emph{process}: one explores, meets the unexpected yet
sought-for, grasps its significance, and comes away with a valuable
outcome~\cite{McCayPeet20151463,Rubin2011,Busch20241110,Makri2012706}.
The find itself is only the occasion for what takes place in the
finder~\cite{Merton1948505}.

The visitor of Section~\ref{sec:introduction} shows all three features
at once: he was actively seeking, the encounter with Debussy was
unplanned, and it clarified what he had been trying to find. The scene
also shows what the definitions leave open: how far his own activity
could reach. Active search begins only once a need has been put into
words the system will take; Taylor traced that path, from a need merely
felt to a question finally asked~\cite{Taylor1968178}. The visitor could
not complete it: such a need cannot, in principle, be specified by the
one who has it~\cite{Belkin198261}.

His search could therefore run on denotation, and no further; where
denotation ended, what remained was chance---the street piano he had not
gone to hear. For the case at hand, the pairing can then be stated
exactly: serendipity is \textit{denotatively unexpected and connotatively
    relevant}. Denotation can be actively explored. Connotation, it seems,
can only be waited for. But could a seeker's own exploration reach one
step further, into the connotative? That is the question of this paper;
before taking it up, let us see how the field has handled denotation and
connotation.


%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsection{Denotation and Connotation}\label{subsec:naming}

Denotation is what a name can hold, and the field long ago built a
superb instrument for it. Bibliographic control is a technology of
naming: the cataloguing tradition provides standardized ways to describe
a work by author, genre, subject, and other facets, from the aims Cutter
set for the catalogue~\cite{Cutter1876} to the facet analysis that now
underlies retrieval well beyond the library~\cite{Broughton200649}.
Denotative attributes are therefore what conventional systems handle
best: they can be named, recorded, indexed, and searched.

Connotation is different. It is the second layer of meaning---the
cultural meanings that attach to a sign beyond what it plainly
denotes~\cite{Rafferty2011283}---and it arises in the encounter, where a
work and a person meet~\cite{Rosenblatt1978}. Three properties make
connotation hard for an information system to represent. First, it has
no stable name. The visitor of Section~\ref{sec:introduction} could
offer only \textit{colors that seem to melt into one another}; indexing
meets the same absence: what a work expresses, Svenonius found, has no
name and therefore no index term~\cite{Svenonius1994600}. Second,
connotation depends on the person who encounters the work. Saracevic set
a \textit{motivational or affective} relevance beside the topical, a
relation of the seeker's own intents and goals~\cite{Saracevic1996201};
for Cosijn and Ingwersen such relevance must be judged from the user's
side, not the assessor's~\cite{Cosijn2000533}. Third, it does not hold
still. A work that fits today's mood may not fit
tomorrow's~\cite{Ross1999783}, and the seeking itself moves with what it
finds~\cite{Bates1989407}: meeting Debussy deepened what the visitor had
been seeking all along.

These properties create a structural problem for information systems.
A system can operate only on representations that remain usable beyond
one person at one moment. Some part of connotation must therefore be
shared---across persons, across moments, and, above all, across works
that denotation keeps apart. Between people such commonality can remain
implicit, resting on a shared background of language, culture, and
embodied experience~\cite{Clark1996,Langacker2008,Hjorland1995400}.
An information system cannot rely on such shared background implicitly;
whatever common structure it uses must be represented in the record
itself. A record means by the symbol alone.

The field has answered at three levels of naming. The first resolves
connotation into finer denotation~\cite{Shatford198639,Saricks1989,Cho2023567}:
even mood can be held on a record, though the finer the labels, the
harder they are to apply consistently~\cite{Saarti200249}. The second
keeps names but replaces discrete labels with continuous dimensions:
Osgood's semantic differential places a work on a set of named
scales~\cite{Osgood1957}, though the scales themselves must still be
chosen in advance. The third gives up named axes altogether and derives
connotative structure from judgments made by many
users~\cite{Rafferty2005,Rafferty2011283}; how to put such collective
judgment in usable form remains open.

Naming is thus a matter of degree: name more, and the representation
becomes easier for a system to handle, but connotation is constrained by
categories chosen in advance. Name less, and connotation remains freer,
but the representation becomes harder to define. How, then, have
information systems answered the seeker who comes looking for the
connotative? That is the question of the next section.

\subsection{Serendipity in Information Systems}\label{subsec:systems}

Table~\ref{tab:quadrants} gives the field's answer in outline: the
two kinds of attribute, crossed with the two active modes of
encounter---searching for what one can specify, and exploring on
one's own. The visitor has already exhausted the left column: its
lower cell is nearly empty, and where denotation ends, search ends
with it.

\begin{table}[t]
    \centering\small
    \caption{Two kinds of attribute crossed with the two active modes
        of encounter.}
    \label{tab:quadrants}
    \begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
        \toprule
                    & Search                     & Exploration                        \\
        \midrule
        Denotative  & bibliographic search, OPAC & faceted navigation                 \\
        \midrule
        Connotative & (nearly empty)             & Book House~\cite{Pejtersen198940}, \\
                    &                            & mood maps                          \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

The right column is where our visitor belongs. Exploration-based
approaches give the user a structured space to navigate: the user
moves, and the system provides the
terrain~\cite{McCayPeet20181,Marchionini200641,Thudt20121461,Beale2007ambient}---and
serendipity, we saw, is insight met by a mind in motion. It is the
column in which the physical library has always excelled. Bj\"orneborn, studying serendipity in physical
libraries, named the affordances that make it so:
\emph{diversifiability}, \emph{traversability}, and
\emph{sensoriability}~\cite{Bjorneborn20171053}---many directions to
differ along, freedom to move among them, and qualities one can sense in
place.

Two lineages of systems have taken up the column. The first builds a
\emph{virtual library}. Pejtersen's Book House furnished the screen
as a house filled with books, and gave each work an artificial
texture to sense: icons evoking what a book holds, so that its feel
could be caught without opening it~\cite{Pejtersen198940,Pejtersen1983230}; its heirs
now walk the aisles in virtual reality~\cite{Lemos201930}. Each work
can be felt in place; the space between works stays unmapped.

The second lineage draws the map. Works are laid out on a plane,
similar works arranged nearer, and the seeker wanders the space
freely~\cite{Pampalk2002570,Knees2020165}. The method is largely
shared: prepare the data the map needs---denotative tags, feature
vectors---and hand it to an existing visualization method; the work
of the lineage lies in preparing data fit for the purpose, and in
the interface built on the map. Even mood can be mapped: experts annotate
the works with mood words, and the layout
follows~\cite{Oliveira2011105,Andjelkovic2019142}.

Beside searching and exploring stands a third mode of encounter,
recommendation, and the table leaves it out by design. There the system
models the user and selects, computationally,
the items worth surprising them
with~\cite{Kotkov2016180,Ziarani2021375,Shrivastava20231,Kunaver2017,Hasan2023933,Chen2019}---a
rich line of research, set aside for one reason: the works arrive, and
our seeker moves. One of its instruments, collaborative filtering,
reaches connotation through no descriptor at all, deriving unnamed
affinity from the listening of many users; what that implies for the
record, we weigh in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

What remains, then, is no longer a question of interface. It is the
question Section~\ref{sec:introduction} argued must come first: the
representation---what the space is made of, before anything is laid
out on a screen.

\subsection{Representation Learning and the Disentanglement
    Challenge}\label{subsec:disentanglement}

What the seeker needs has been taking shape over the last three
subsections. What he seeks is connotative, and no denotative term
will hold it; so the space that serves him must be one he can explore
by moving. And were the works around him arranged by connotative
nearness, he could seek what he cannot name---feeling the works as he
goes, walking toward what resonates.

The problem is now plain: to arrange works by connotative
kinship. The kinship, moreover, must hold across denotative
difference---otherwise no leap could carry the seeker from one work
to its kin in another genre. And what we are given is the content
data, where connotative information lives; but it lives there
entangled with the denotative, and left as they are, the two collapse
into a single distance. They must be pulled apart.

In machine learning, this is precisely the problem that representation learning
names~\cite{Bengio20131798}. A representation in which distinct
factors of variation occupy separable directions is called
\textit{disentangled}~\cite{Higgins2017}; what we seek is a
representation of the content data in which denotation and connotation are
disentangled. In such a space \textit{denotatively
    unexpected and connotatively relevant}
(Section~\ref{subsec:serendipity_process}) acquires
coordinates---denotatively distant, yet connotatively close---and the
space itself, told as a library, is the library with rabbit holes of
Section~\ref{sec:introduction}.

Constructing such a disentangled representation
is a recognized open challenge. In the unsupervised setting it is provably ill-posed: for any disentangled
solution, infinitely many entangled ones generate the same
observations~\cite{Locatello20197247}. Some \textit{inductive bias}---a
structural assumption about the space---is therefore unavoidable; the question
is not whether to adopt one, but which one suits the pairing at hand.
Section~\ref{sec:framework} paints the space; Section~\ref{sec:mathematics}
shows that, once the scope of the data is fixed, an appropriate bias follows
naturally, in the form of optimal transport.
