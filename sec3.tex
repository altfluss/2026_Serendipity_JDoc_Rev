%%%%% Section 3 A Library with Rabbit Holes %%%%%
\section{A Library with Rabbit Holes}\label{sec:framework}

Return to the visitor of Section~\ref{sec:introduction}. What he could name
was little: a title (\textit{Kind of Blue}), its performer (Miles Davis), its
genre (jazz). Each of these fits a field on a record, and each is denotation.
The rest of what he carried fits no field: the sound of the instruments, the
mood, the atmosphere---quiet, with feeling in it, colors melting into one
another. That rest is connotation.

From here we simplify: let the library carry these three descriptors and no
others. To look for works beyond Miles Davis, then, he has no descriptor but
genre. What exploration space must the library give him, so that he can seek
what he is after? That question is the subject of this section.

He can also reach the contents themselves---to listen and see for himself. So
the \emph{content data} drawn from those contents is available to us as well.
Descriptors and content data together we call the \emph{collection data}. Our
aim is then to build the exploration space from the collection data.

%%% Subsection 3.1 The Multi-Floor Library %%%%%
\subsection{The Multi-Floor Library: The Disentangled
    Form}\label{subsec:multi_floor_library}

Consider first what descriptors alone can build: works gathered by genre, one
floor to each in a library, and within a floor shelved in performer and title
order. The visitor asks for the jazz floor and finds \textit{Kind of Blue} and
the other works of Miles Davis. There his intention runs out. Shelf order
tells him nothing of what a work is like, so he cannot move even along the
jazz floor toward what he is after; on the classical floor he can only take
works down at random.

Suppose now that each floor can arrange its works by connotative closeness:
beside any work stand the works that feel like it. Near \textit{Kind of Blue}
he then finds other works of similar mood, and walking away from it he finds
the mood changing gradually.

We do not ask the floor to hold every connotation. We ask for the principal
ones---those that stand next after the denotation of genre, and that most
visitors feel in common: instruments, mood, and their like. We call such a
floor a \textbf{principal connotative space}. For now we postulate that we
have one. We do not ask whether it exists, or whether collection data can
give it. We assume only that every work carries a unique coordinate in it.
That much already lets him explore the jazz floor.

Assume further that this coordinate system is shared by every genre. From the
coordinate of \textit{Kind of Blue} on the jazz floor, moving to the same
coordinate on the classical floor brings him a work of the same principal
connotation (Figure~\ref{fig:two_views}).

His reach now runs past genre to the whole library. He set out holding nothing
but \textit{Kind of Blue}, Miles Davis, and jazz, and with no words to help him
further, and yet the whole building is open to him.

This is the \textbf{multi-floor library with rabbit holes} that we propose.
Genre puts each work on its floor; the principal connotative space puts it in
its place on that floor. A rabbit hole is a vertical elevator, leaping to the
same coordinate on another floor. So he can open his own way from Miles Davis
to Debussy.

This library meets Bj\"orneborn's three affordances
(Section~\ref{subsec:systems})~\citep{Bjorneborn20171053}.
\textbf{Diversifiability}: beyond what the descriptors carry, the whole
library opens to him as a multi-dimensional space.
\textbf{Traversability}: he can traverse that space with intent, choosing what
to hold fixed and what to vary. Walking a floor holds the genre and crosses
connotation; entering a rabbit hole holds the connotation and crosses genre.
\textbf{Sensoriability}: he can play a work, listen, and feel what connotation
its position carries---and, before he listens, foresee what the other works
there will be like.

Foreseeing is what affords his exploration. It lets him set out with a clear
intent and an expectation of what lies ahead.

%%% Subsection 3.2 Seeking Serendipity in the Library %%%%%
\subsection{Seeking Serendipity in the Library}\label{subsec:seeking}

Let the visitor into this space, seeking as ever ``something like
Miles Davis.'' What he carries, the coordinates let us finally write
down: somewhere within him is a connotative coordinate of his
own---call it $v_\text{seeker}$. He cannot state it;
Section~\ref{subsec:serendipity_process} told us why: a need of this
kind is visceral before it is verbalized, and cannot be specified by
its owner. He does not, in fact, know that $v_\text{seeker}$ exists. What he can
do is stand before a work and feel whether it resonates---whether the
work's coordinate lies near his own. That sense of affinity is his;
the library has no access to it.

He begins where anyone begins: with what he can name. The jazz floor
he can ask for, and once there, he walks. Under his feet the
connotative coordinate varies continuously; near Miles Davis the
resonance strengthens, and he slows, and stops. Call the place where
he stands $v_\text{work}$. Everything so far he could have foreseen (R4):
within a floor, one moves toward more of what one already feels. So
far this is seeking, and the space serves it.

Then he steps into the hole at $v_\text{work}$. The leap holds
$v_\text{work}$ fixed and lets the genre change entirely; he surfaces on
the classical floor, where nothing nameable survived the
passage---genre, instrument, period, all different---and yet, before
Debussy, the resonance holds. The field's oldest pairing,
\textit{unexpected and relevant}~\cite{Kotkov2016180}, becomes readable as
two coordinates: unexpected with respect to every denotation,
relevant in the one connotation the hole held fixed.

And what he finds is more than a piano piece. On the jazz floor the
resonance might still have been jazz-ness, or Miles-ness; the leap
stripped those accounts away, and what still resonates can only be
the coordinate the hole held. At that moment the visitor discovers
that what he had been circling answers to something in himself---that
$v_\text{seeker}$ exists. ``\textit{So this is what I was looking for}'':
the moment of serendipity, \textit{a mix of unexpectedness and
    insight}, as Makri and Blandford heard it~\cite{Makri2012684}---the
unexpectedness of the instant before, the insight of the instant
after.

And the moment is not the end of the walk. He can act on the encounter---ride
the same hole to a third floor, turn back, walk a little and leap
again---Merton's ``unanticipated, anomalous and strategic
datum''~\cite{Merton1948505}: strategic, because here a finding becomes a plan.
The first leap told him that $v_\text{seeker}$ exists; those that follow tell
him what it is---not a work in another genre, but a structure, unnamed, running
across them all.

The division of labor is exact. The library measures affinity only
between works; that is its geometry. The affinity between $v_\text{work}$
and the visitor's own $v_\text{seeker}$ no library can measure, for it
lives in him. The space does not contain serendipity; it gives
serendipity somewhere to happen.


%%% Subsection 3.3 The Multi-Galaxy Curved Universe %%%%%
\subsection{The Multi-Galaxy Curved Universe: The Same Works in Their
    Native Form}\label{subsec:multi_galaxy}

The library is the form we seek; the works do not arrive in it. They arrive as
collection data---descriptors and content data, paired work by work. The
descriptors give the genre, and with it the floor. The content data comes
embedded by preprocessing into a vector, often, of hundreds or thousands of
dimensions. Section~\ref{sec:introduction} has already shown that space: works
as stars, genres as loose galaxies, the whole a multi-galaxy curved universe
(Figure~\ref{fig:two_views}b). What it could not yet say is why this universe
cannot simply be walked.

This universe cannot be walked because it has no meaningful axes---not merely
because it has too many. Hundreds of dimensions do offer more directions than a
visitor could hold in mind, but thinning them would not help: the axes are
artifacts of preprocessing, aligned with nothing a visitor feels. Moving along
one, he cannot tell what would change and what would stay (R4). Connotation is
present here---the passage from Miles Davis to Debussy runs through this
space---but as a curved trajectory that no axis follows: real, but not
navigable.

The universe and the library are two views of one structure, separated only by a
coordinate system (Figure~\ref{fig:OT-FB}). The works are the same, threaded by
the same holes; what differs is whether the coordinates carry meaning. In the
library they do: hold one fixed, and what it names holds still, and the passage
from floor to floor runs straight. In the universe that meaning is spread across
every axis at once, and the same passage curves across the dark between
galaxies. Straight in the disentangled view, curved in the entangled one. To
pass from the one view to the other is to find that system.

Whether it can be found is a threefold question. Can a coordinate system
of this kind be laid over a given collection at all? If it can, is it
the only one? And if so, do its coordinates truly express connotation?
The next section answers all three.

\subsection{Representation Learning and the Disentanglement
    Challenge}\label{subsec:disentanglement}

The question just posed can be restated as a problem about the data.
What we are given is the content data of the works themselves, and it
carries both aspects: the denotative, which descriptors already name, and the connotative, which they do not. That content can carry
what no descriptor does, the scene of
Section~\ref{sec:introduction} has already shown: what stopped the
visitor in the station was sound alone, with no record in sight.
But in the data the two lie mixed, and left as they are, they collapse into a single distance. They
must be pulled apart.

In machine learning, this is precisely the problem that representation learning
names~\cite{Bengio20131798}. A representation in which distinct
factors of variation occupy separable directions is called
\textit{disentangled}~\cite{Higgins2017}; what we seek is a
representation of the content data in which denotation and connotation are
disentangled. In such a space \textit{denotatively
    unexpected and connotatively relevant}
(Section~\ref{subsec:serendipity_process}) acquires
coordinates---denotatively distant, yet connotatively close---and the
space itself, told as a library, is the library with rabbit holes of
Section~\ref{sec:introduction}.

Constructing such a disentangled representation is a recognized open challenge.
In the unsupervised setting it is provably ill-posed: for any disentangled
solution, infinitely many entangled ones generate the same
observations~\cite{Locatello20197247}. Some \textit{inductive bias}---a
structural assumption about the space---is therefore unavoidable; the question
is not whether to adopt one, but which one suits the pairing at hand.
Section~\ref{sec:framework} paints the space; Section~\ref{sec:mathematics}
shows that, once we state what the connotation coordinate should express, an
appropriate bias follows naturally, in the form of optimal transport.
