\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Let us begin with a scene that any music librarian will recognize: a visitor
comes to the reference desk, looking for ``\textit{something like Miles Davis's
    \textup{Kind of Blue}---that atmosphere I cannot quite name.}'' The librarian
asks for something more specific; the visitor tries to find words, and finally
offers: ``\textit{quiet, but with feeling in it; the colors melt into one
    another; nothing is ever stated outright.}'' A visitor who gets this far is
doing well---most stop at the first sentence. The librarian understands the
request, or feels she does. But consider what the catalogue gives her to work
with. From that one record it retrieves a genre (jazz), an instrument (trumpet),
a year (1959), a country (the United States); a search on these facets returns
hard bop, fusion, and a crowd of other trumpeters---almost none of it what he
wants. Some weeks later, in a train station, a street pianist is playing
something that stops him where he stands---Debussy's piano music---and he knows:
``\textit{so this is what I was looking for.}''

This episode shows several faces of serendipity at once. First, no path led
him there. He could not have known where the thing he sought was shelved; he
had no keyword the catalogue would accept; the librarian, for all her skill,
had nothing to point to. Yet the moment he came upon it, he knew with a
certainty that needed no argument: \textit{this is it}. Serendipity research
has long compressed these two moments into a phrase---\textit{unexpected and
    relevant}~\citep{Kotkov2016180}.

Second, before the encounter and after it, no single word was enough. Genre,
instrument, period, country---these are \emph{denotative} attributes, each
carried whole by a single word. Between Miles Davis and Debussy, every one of
them changed: jazz became classical, trumpet piano, the 1950s the 1900s,
America France; a performer became a composer. Not one catalogued attribute
survived the passage---yet the visitor's criterion was met. What he sought,
then, was an attribute of another kind; call it \emph{connotative}: one that
no single word carries whole. Words point toward it; they do not hold it.
``\textit{Quiet, but with feeling in it}'' comes close, further words come
closer, and so does pointing at kindred works---ever nearer, never arriving.

Third, what he found was more than a piano piece. He said, ``\textit{so this
    is what \emph{I} was looking for}''---not ``\textit{what a fine piece.}''
What he discovered was his own criterion---something he could not have stated
before the encounter---and, with it, the family of works that answer to it.
\citet{Makri2012684} found the same thing in researchers' accounts of
serendipity: \textit{a mix of unexpectedness and insight}---here, insight
into his own seeking.

Last, consider the librarian. She understood the request, or felt she did,
and still could do nothing with it; in the end the visitor had to find the
piece by his own wandering. Yet when he returned with a name, she served him
in a minute: Debussy had been there all along, and Debussy led on to Ravel.
Wherever a name reaches, a library serves. The scene called for exploration,
not search. And a library ordered by what can be named gave that exploration
nothing to hold on to. So the question stands: for a visitor who seeks a
connotation---something written in no field of any record---what must a
library be, so that he can go looking for it?

Our answer is: a \textbf{multi-floor library with rabbit holes}.\footnote{In
    \textit{Alice's Adventures in Wonderland}, Alice follows a rabbit down a hole
    into another world.} In this library, works are classified by denotation,
floor by floor---a floor for each genre, say. An ordinary library's ways
remain: searching by name, browsing by name. But everywhere in the stacks
there are rabbit holes---entrances that let a visitor leap to another floor.
Each hole holds one connotative closeness, and runs vertically through the
whole building, opening onto every floor. If a work at hand feels close to
what one is after---Miles Davis, on the jazz floor---the visitor may leap
then and there; he will surface on the classical floor, near Debussy. Every
hole promises this much: the point of departure decides the point of arrival.
What waits there, it does not promise; the visitor may find a serendipitous
encounter---or may not, and turn back. Either way, the finding is the
visitor's own; no library can deliver it. The design changes something
quieter: exploration with nothing to hold on to becomes exploration along
rabbit holes. Serendipity cannot be handed over. It can be sought.

A library of this kind is a matter of \emph{representation}, not of interface
design. Exploration needs ground to move on, and the representation lays it:
coordinates give each work a \emph{place}, and a space of places can be walked.
On that ground many interfaces can be built, and none can surface what the
ground does not hold~\citep{Munzner2009921}.

Where does such a representation come from? The descriptors give each work
its floor: genre, instrument, period, country. They cannot give it a place
within that floor, for connotation is written in no field of any record. But
the works themselves reach us as recordings, or as the words written about
them, and this \emph{content data} carries what the descriptors leave unsaid.
Our aim, then, takes its working form: to build the multi-floor library with
rabbit holes from the collection data---descriptors for the floors, content
data for the holes. The representation we seek is a \emph{geometry}, and it
is the content data that must determine it.

In this work, we propose two geometric objects. The first is the
\textbf{Optimal Transport Fiber Bundle} (OT-FB), which defines the library as
a whole: floors and rabbit holes take geometric form, given by the optimal
transport that carries the works of one genre onto those of another. The
second is what the rabbit holes make up: a \textbf{principal connotative
    space}, the form connotation takes when it becomes geometry. It is to
connotation what principal component analysis (PCA) is to data. The geometry
is essentially unique, and it can be learned from an actual collection.

This paper never names the affinity between Miles Davis and Debussy. It gives
the affinity a form instead: geometry. Held as coordinates, the connotation
remains unnamed and yet gains a place where it can be felt. Serendipity is
felt as closeness, and closeness is a matter of place. This is what the
rabbit holes offer: a passage that no name reaches, and somewhere for
serendipity to happen.

\paragraph{Paper structure.}
Section~\ref{sec:serendipity} reviews serendipity research---its process
models, the classical theory of relevance and of naming, and the affordances
of the physical library---and asks what an information space must give a
visitor who seeks what no name reaches.
Section~\ref{sec:framework} presents the vision: the multi-floor library with
rabbit holes, and the same works seen in the form the data give them.
Section~\ref{sec:mathematics} gives the vision its definite form: the OT-FB
geometry. Section~\ref{sec:computation} derives the objective function, shows
the solution is essentially unique, and learns the bundle from the collection
data. Section~\ref{sec:demonstration} demonstrates the bundle on two
collections: a music library built from sound, and a book library with two
kinds of rabbit holes. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} reflects on what the
pursuit revealed, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes: the boundary of
denotation is not the limit of seeking but the ground one leaps from.
