\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Let us begin with a scene that any music librarian will recognize: a visitor
comes to the reference desk, looking for ``\textit{something like Miles
    Davis---that atmosphere I cannot quite name.}'' The librarian asks for something
more specific; the visitor searches for words, and finally offers:
``\textit{quiet, but with feeling in it; impressionistic; colors that seem to
    melt into one another.}'' A visitor who gets this far is doing well---most never
move past the first sentence. The librarian understands the request, or feels
she does. But consider what the catalogue gives her to work with. From ``Miles
Davis'' it retrieves a genre (jazz), an instrument (trumpet), a period (the
1950s and 60s), a country (the United States); a search on these facets returns
hard bop, fusion, and a quantity of other trumpeters, most of it not what the
visitor wants. Some weeks later, by another route---browsing a shelf, or a
friend's recommendation---he comes upon Debussy's piano
music,\footnote{A kinship often remarked: the modal language of Miles
    Davis's \emph{Kind of Blue}, and the pianism of Bill Evans, its pianist,
    have both been discussed in relation to Debussy and
    Ravel~\cite{TODO-musicology}.} and thinks:
``\textit{so this is what I was looking for.}''

This short episode shows several faces of serendipity at once. First,
the visitor could not have known in advance where the thing he sought was
shelved, and he had no keyword the catalogue could receive; the librarian, for
all her skill, had nothing to point to. Yet the moment he came upon it, he
knew with a certainty that needed no argument: \textit{this is it}. Serendipity
research has long compressed this pairing into a phrase---``unexpected and
relevant''~\cite{Kotkov2016180}---which recurs, in varied wordings, across
the field~\cite{Ziarani2021375,Chen2019,Hasan2023933,Nahm2023}.
Second, neither before the encounter nor after it could he say in a single
word what the thing is. Genre, instrument, period, country---these are
\emph{denotative} attributes, each carried whole by a single word. And
between Miles Davis and Debussy, every one of them changed: jazz became
classical, trumpet piano, the 1950s the 1900s, America France; a performer
became a composer. Not one catalogued attribute survived the passage---yet
the visitor's criterion was met. What he seeks, then, is an attribute of
another kind; call it \emph{connotative}: one that no single word says
whole. Not that it lies beyond words. ``Quiet, but with
feeling in it'' comes close, further words come closer, and so does pointing
at kindred works---ever nearer, never arriving. Third,
what he found was not a piano piece. He did not say ``what a fine piece''; he
said ``this is what \emph{I} was looking for.'' The object of his discovery
was his own criterion---something he could not have stated before the
encounter---and, with it, the family of works that answer to it. Makri and
Blandford heard the same thing in researchers' accounts of serendipity: at
its core, ``a mix of unexpectedness and
insight''~\cite{Makri2012part1}---here, insight into his own seeking.

Last, consider the librarian. She understood the request, or felt she did,
and still could do nothing with it; in the end the visitor had to find the
piece himself, by his own wandering. What the scene called for was not search
but exploration---and a library ordered by what can be named gave that
exploration nothing to hold on to. So the question poses itself: for a
visitor who seeks a connotation, something written in no field of any record,
what would a library have to be, so that he could go looking for it?

Our answer, given here in advance of everything that supports it, is: \emph{a
    library with rabbit holes}.\footnote{In \textit{Alice's Adventures in
        Wonderland}, Alice follows a rabbit down a hole into another world.} In this
library, works are classified by denotation, floor by floor---a floor for
each genre, say---so everything an ordinary library affords is still
afforded: searching by name, browsing by name. But everywhere in the stacks
there are rabbit holes---entrances that let a visitor leap to another
floor. Each hole holds one connotative closeness---one of those qualities
that no single word carries---and runs vertically through the whole
building, opening onto every floor. If a work at hand feels close to what one
is after---Miles Davis, on the jazz floor---the visitor may leap then and
there; coming out on the classical floor, he will surface near Debussy.
This much every hole promises: the point of departure decides the point of
arrival. What waits there, it does not promise; the visitor may find a
serendipitous encounter---or may not, and turn back. Either way, the
finding is the visitor's own; no library can deliver it. What the design changes is quieter: exploration with nothing to
hold on to becomes exploration along rabbit holes. Serendipity, which cannot
be handed over, becomes something that can be sought.

A library of this kind is not a matter of interface design; it is a matter of
\emph{representation}. What it gives each work is not one more denotative
label but a \emph{place}: coordinates in a space that can be walked. The
coordinates are continuous, as connotation is, and they name nothing---a
coordinate says ``here,'' not ``melancholic.'' And the representation must come
first, before any interface, for no interface can surface what the space beneath
it does not already encode~\cite{Blair1985289,Furnas1987964}.

Seen from the data, the same structure wears another face. As they come to
us---as recordings, or as the words gathered about them---the works are
points in a high-dimensional space, scattered like stars in a universe.
Neighboring stars
are works with similar features, and denotation---genre, above all---gathers
the stars into loose clusters: galaxies. But in this universe, a straight
line means nothing. The rabbit hole, which in the library runs vertically
from floor to floor, here bends across the dark between galaxies: a
\textbf{multi-galaxy curved universe}, of which the \textbf{multi-floor
    library} is the \emph{disentangled} form. To walk a floor is to hold
the denotation fixed and move through everything it leaves unsaid; to leap
a rabbit hole is to hold one connotation fixed and cross denotations
entirely. Curved in the entangled universe, straight in the disentangled
library, the passage is one and the same.

At this point, the representation of connotation has become a problem of
mathematical formulation. Our answer is the \textbf{Optimal Transport Fiber
    Bundle} (OT-FB): a \emph{disentangled} representation space in which floors
and rabbit holes are geometric objects, determined by the optimal transport
that carries the works of one genre onto those of another. The mathematics
guarantees that the representation is essentially unique---whoever computes
it obtains the same coordinates---and computation gives it reality: the
bundle can be learned from an actual collection.

The affinity between Miles Davis and Debussy is never named by a single
word in this paper, and never needs to be. It receives a definite form instead: held as geometry,
as coordinates that do not name, the connotation remains unnamed and yet
gains a place where it can be felt. This is what the rabbit holes offer---passages
through which an explorer can be led by a resonance that no name reaches, an
exploration in which serendipity has somewhere to happen. Our contribution is
the representation that makes such passage possible---and, with it, an
account of why the felt closeness of serendipity resists reduction to
denotative attributes, and of what a representation must hold instead.

\paragraph{Paper structure.}
Section~\ref{sec:lineages} reviews serendipity research---its process
models, the affordances of the physical library, and the classical theory
of relevance and of naming---and asks what an information space must hold
if serendipity is to be sought in it: a \emph{disentangled information
    representation}. Section~\ref{sec:framework} presents the vision---the
multi-floor library, the disentangled form of the entangled multi-galaxy
curved universe. Section~\ref{sec:mathematics} shows how Optimal Transport
Fiber Bundle (OT-FB) geometry realizes this vision, and why the resulting
coordinates are essentially unique. Section~\ref{sec:computation} turns the
geometry into computation: the bundle is learned from the works themselves
and the names they are given. Section~\ref{sec:demonstration} demonstrates
the representation on an actual music collection.
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} reflects on what the pursuit revealed---the
necessity of disentanglement, the fitness of optimal transport as an
inductive bias, and what the geometry returns to libraries and to the
theory of relevance---and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} closes the circle
opened by Miles Davis and Debussy.

    This scene is our point of departure, and we take it apart before building
    on it. First, everything the catalogue records has changed. Jazz has become
    classical, trumpet piano, the 1950s the 1900s, America France; a performer
    has even become a composer. Not one catalogued attribute survived the
    passage---and yet the visitor's criterion was met. Second, the affinity is
    not a private fancy. The kinship between the music around Miles Davis and
    impressionist harmony has been remarked many times: the modal language of
    \emph{Kind of Blue}, Bill Evans's pianism, and Gil Evans's orchestration
    have all been discussed in relation to Debussy and
    Ravel~\cite{TODO-musicology}. What the visitor sensed lies \emph{in the
        works}; it is simply written in none of the catalogue's fields. Third, what
    the visitor discovered was not a piano piece. He did not say ``what a fine
    piece''; he said ``this is what \emph{I} was looking for.'' The object of
    his discovery was his own criterion---something he could not have stated
    before the encounter. Fourth, he had indicated his need in two ways: by
    pointing at a work (``like Miles Davis''), and by heaping up partially apt
    words (``quiet, with feeling, melting colors''). Neither is a subject
    term, and the catalogue has a field for neither.

    Wilson distinguished two powers at which bibliographic control can
    aim~\cite{Wilson1968}: \emph{descriptive} power, the power to find
    everything that bears on a topic, and \emph{exploitative} power, the power
    to put recorded knowledge to one's own best use. What the cataloguing
    tradition built to a high finish---from Cutter's objects through facet
    analysis---is the former; the latter has been left, for the most part, to
    the skill of readers and librarians. Our visitor is asking for the latter:
    not what a work is \emph{about}, but how it \emph{feels}.

    Two kinds of attribute must therefore be told apart, and the pair will
    anchor everything that follows. \emph{Denotative} attributes can be
    expressed as sets of mutually exclusive labels and implemented as facets:
    genre, period, creator, instrument, country. \emph{Connotative} attributes
    cannot. They resist a single name, come in degrees, are many-dimensional,
    interfere with one another, and take form in the encounter between work and
    listener. What Miles Davis and Debussy share is of this second kind. Cross
    the pair with the familiar division between known-item search and
    exploratory browsing, and four quadrants appear
    (Table~\ref{tab:quadrants}). Faceted navigation is exploratory yet entirely
    denotative, so the customary opposition of ``facets versus exploration''
    misses the point: what facets fail to carry is not exploration but the
    unnamed. And the quadrant of connotative search---retrieving a work by a
    quality no one can name---is nearly empty. Why it is empty will become
    clear in Section~\ref{subsec:third}.

    This problem is not new to library and information science. It has been
    worked on from three directions, by three lineages that rarely cite one
    another. How ``quiet, with feeling, melting colors'' might be written into
    a bibliographic record is a question of \emph{representation}
    (Section~\ref{subsec:first}). That the visitor could not say what he sought
    until he had found it is a question about \emph{the seeker}
    (Section~\ref{subsec:second}). How a system might receive ``like Miles
    Davis'' is a question about \emph{the means of access}
    (Section~\ref{subsec:third}). Section~\ref{sec:lineages} follows each
    lineage to its endpoint; laid over one another, the three endpoints
    determine what is required almost uniquely. It is not another interface. It
    is a \emph{representation}---something written into records, outliving its
    maker, shared across libraries---for qualities that have no name; for no
    interface can surface what the representation beneath it does not already
    encode~\cite{Blair1985289,Furnas1987964}. Section~\ref{sec:framework}
    states the proposal: a coordinate system in which a work's nameable
    properties and its unnamed qualities occupy separate coordinates, the
    unnamed ones serving as \emph{headings without names}---and there
    serendipity, too, acquires a precise sense: the seeker's recognition that
    one of their own unnamed targets is expressed by such a coordinate.
    Section~\ref{sec:mathematics} supplies the mathematics on which the
    proposal leans---the metric under which the coordinate system is unique, so
    that whoever computes it obtains the same one; this, we will argue, is the
    condition for coordinates to serve as authority data.
    Section~\ref{sec:computation} shows that the coordinates can be learned
    from a collection and its catalogue metadata,
    Section~\ref{sec:demonstration} demonstrates this on real data,
    Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses what changes for libraries, and
    Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.
