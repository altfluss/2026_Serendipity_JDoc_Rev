\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Let us begin with a scene that any music librarian will recognize: a visitor
comes to the reference desk, looking for ``\textit{something like Miles
    Davis---that atmosphere I cannot quite name.}'' The librarian asks for
something more specific; the visitor tries to find words, and finally offers:
``\textit{quiet, but with feeling in it; the colors melt into one another;
    nothing is ever stated outright.}'' A visitor who gets this far is doing
well---most stop at the first sentence. The librarian understands the request,
or feels she does. But consider what the catalogue gives her to work with. From
``Miles Davis'' it retrieves a genre (jazz), an instrument (trumpet), a period
(the 1950s and 60s), a country (the United States); a search on these facets
returns hard bop, fusion, and a crowd of other trumpeters---almost none of it
what he wants. Some weeks later, in a train station, a street pianist is playing
something that stops him where he stands---Debussy's piano music---and he knows:
``\textit{so this is what I was looking for.}''

This episode shows several faces of serendipity at once. First, no path led
him there. He could not have known where the thing he sought was shelved; he
had no keyword the catalogue would accept; the librarian, for all her skill,
had nothing to point to. Yet the moment he came upon it, he knew with a
certainty that needed no argument: \textit{this is it}. Serendipity research
has long compressed these two moments into a phrase---\textit{unexpected and
    relevant}~\cite{Kotkov2016180}---which recurs across the field in varied
wordings~\cite{Ziarani2021375,Chen2019,Hasan2023933,Nahm2023}.

Second, neither before the encounter nor after it could he say in a single
word what the thing is. Genre, instrument, period, country---these are
\emph{denotative} attributes, each carried whole by a single word. And
between Miles Davis and Debussy, every one of them changed: jazz became
classical, trumpet piano, the 1950s the 1900s, America France; a performer
became a composer. Not one catalogued attribute survived the passage---yet
the visitor's criterion was met. What he seeks, then, is an attribute of
another kind; call it \emph{connotative}: one that no single word says
whole. Not that it lies beyond words. ``Quiet, but with
feeling in it'' comes close, further words come closer, and so does pointing
at kindred works---ever nearer, never arriving.

Third, what he found was not a piano piece. He did not say ``what a fine
piece''; he said ``so this is what \emph{I} was looking for.'' The object of his
discovery was his own criterion---something he could not have stated before the
encounter---and, with it, the family of works that answer to it. Makri and
Blandford heard the same thing in researchers' accounts of serendipity:
\textit{a mix of unexpectedness and insight}~\cite{Makri2012684}---here, insight
into his own seeking.

Last, consider the librarian. She understood the request, or felt she did,
and still could do nothing with it; in the end the visitor had to find the
piece himself, by his own wandering. Yet when he returned with a name, she
served him in a minute: Debussy had been there all along, and Debussy led
on to Ravel. Wherever a name reaches, a library serves. What the scene
called for was not search but exploration---and a library ordered by what
can be named gave that exploration nothing to hold on to. So the question
poses itself: for a visitor who seeks a connotation, something written in
no field of any record, what would a library have to be, so that he could
go looking for it?

Our answer, given here in advance of everything that supports it, is: a
\textbf{multi-floor library with rabbit holes}.\footnote{In \textit{Alice's
        Adventures in Wonderland}, Alice follows a rabbit down a hole into another
    world.} In this library, works are classified by denotation, floor by
floor---a floor for each genre, say---so everything an ordinary library
affords is still afforded: searching by name, browsing by name. But
everywhere in the stacks there are rabbit holes---entrances that let a
visitor leap to another floor. Each hole holds one connotative closeness,
and runs vertically through the whole building, opening onto every floor. If
a work at hand feels close to what one is after---Miles Davis, on the jazz
floor---the visitor may leap then and there; he will surface on the
classical floor, near Debussy. This much every hole promises: the point of
departure decides the point of arrival. What waits there, it does not
promise; the visitor may find a serendipitous encounter---or may not, and
turn back. Either way, the finding is the visitor's own; no library can
deliver it. What the design changes is quieter: exploration with nothing to
hold on to becomes exploration along rabbit holes. Serendipity, which cannot
be handed over, becomes something that can be sought.

A library of this kind is not a matter of interface design; it is a matter of
\emph{representation}. What it gives each work is not one more denotative
label but a \emph{place}: coordinates in a space that can be walked. The
coordinates are continuous, as connotation is, and they name nothing---a
coordinate says ``here,'' not ``melancholic.'' And the representation must come
first, before any interface, for no interface can surface what the space beneath
it does not already encode~\cite{Blair1985289,Furnas1987964}.

Seen from the content data, the same structure wears another face. The works
reach us as recordings, or as the words written about them; either way, they are
points in a high-dimensional space, scattered like stars in a universe.
Neighboring stars are works with similar features, and denotation---genre, above
all---gathers the stars into loose clusters: galaxies. But in this universe, a
straight line means nothing. The rabbit hole, which in the library runs straight
from floor to floor, here curves across the dark between galaxies: a
\textbf{multi-galaxy curved universe}, of which the multi-floor library with
rabbit holes is the \emph{disentangled} form. To walk a floor is to hold the
denotation fixed and move through everything it leaves unsaid; to leap a rabbit
hole is to hold one connotation fixed and cross denotations entirely. Curved in
the entangled universe, straight in the disentangled library, the passage is one
and the same.

At this point, the representation of connotation has become a problem of
mathematical formulation. Our answer is the \textbf{Optimal Transport Fiber
    Bundle} (OT-FB): a \emph{disentangled} representation space in which floors and
rabbit holes are geometric objects, determined by the optimal transport that
carries the works of one genre onto those of another. The mathematics guarantees
that the representation is essentially unique, and computation gives it reality:
the fiber bundle, namely the bundle of rabbit holes, can be learned from an
actual collection. OT-FB, then, gives the rabbit holes their concrete
definition: they make up a \textbf{principal connotation space}. As principal
component analysis (PCA) extracts the principal dimensions of data, the bundle
extracts from the content data the principal connotations of a collection.

The affinity between Miles Davis and Debussy is never named by a single
word in this paper, and never needs to be. It receives a definite form
instead: geometry. Held as coordinates, the connotation remains unnamed
and yet gains a place where it can be felt. This is what the rabbit holes offer---passages
through which an explorer can be led by a resonance that no name reaches, an
exploration in which serendipity has somewhere to happen. Our contribution is
the representation that makes such passage possible---and, with it, an
account of why the felt closeness of serendipity resists reduction to
denotative attributes, and of what a representation must hold instead.

\paragraph{Paper structure.}
Section~\ref{sec:serendipity} reviews serendipity research---its process models,
the classical theory of relevance and of naming, and the affordances of the
physical library---and asks what an information space must hold: a
\emph{disentangled information representation}. Section~\ref{sec:framework}
presents the vision: the multi-floor library with rabbit holes, the disentangled
form of the entangled multi-galaxy curved universe.
Section~\ref{sec:mathematics} realizes the vision as Optimal Transport Fiber
Bundle (OT-FB) geometry, whose coordinates are essentially unique.
Section~\ref{sec:computation} learns the bundle from the collection data, and
Section~\ref{sec:demonstration} demonstrates it on an actual music collection.
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} reflects on what the pursuit revealed, and
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes what it all means for the seeking of
serendipity: at the boundary of denotation, a dimension one never had comes into
view.
