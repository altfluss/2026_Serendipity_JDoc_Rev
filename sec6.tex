%%%%% Section 6. Demonstration with real data %%%%%
\section{Demonstration}\label{sec:demonstration}

Sections~\ref{sec:framework}--\ref{sec:computation} built the library
in concept, in mathematics, and in computation. What remains is to
build one. This section constructs the OT-FB from two actual
collections: a music collection, whose content data come from sound
alone, and a collection of children's books, which carries two kinds
of content data and so receives two bundles of rabbit holes at once.
We first state what the construction asks of the content data, then
build the two libraries in turn.


%%% Subsection 6.1 %%%
\subsection{What the Construction Asks of the Content
    Data}\label{subsec:content_data}

The OT-FB inherits its geometry from the content space. Everything the
construction of Sections~\ref{sec:mathematics}
and~\ref{sec:computation} rests on is measured there: the proximity
assumption (A2) and the transport cost are both stated in the
Euclidean distance of $\mathcal{X}$---the general-purpose inner
product that Section~\ref{sec:framework} asked the content data to
carry. Which works a rabbit hole joins is therefore decided by what
counts as close in $\mathcal{X}$.

The content space must accordingly be \emph{unfolded}: wherever works
are kindred, Euclidean closeness must report it, and wherever works
lie close, kinship must be what brought them there. The demand falls
on neighborhoods, not on the space at large; the space may curve as
it pleases, so long as nearness means kinship. Raw signals do not
satisfy this. Two kindred performances may lie far apart as
waveforms, and two kindred books as word counts, while accidents of
encoding sit side by side; a bundle built on such a space would dig
its holes along those accidents.

Deep embeddings supply spaces of the required kind. Foundation
encoders are trained on large corpora precisely so that semantic
closeness becomes geometric closeness, and their embedding spaces are
unfolded in the sense we need. For sound, we use CLAP~\cite{Wu2023},
a joint audio--text embedding: the music library of
Section~\ref{subsec:music_library} is built from sound alone, with no
words about the works entering the content data.

When the content data are words about the works, an LLM text
embedding plays the same role. The words need not come from a
controlled vocabulary: a ready-made description---an abstract, a
synopsis---serves as it stands, and where none exists, one is
composed from the tags users have freely given---``\textit{this work
    carries the following tags: $t_1, t_2, \ldots$}''---and embedded
whole. Nor is a word any longer a label. \textit{Chill} and
\textit{relaxed} lie close together in the embedding, as they do in
use. And words do not merely add up: \textit{beautiful} and
\textit{sad}, given together, make a mood of their own---\textit{beautiful
    and sad}---which the embedding reads whole. This is, in the end,
the visitor's own method---``\textit{quiet, but with feeling in it}''
was already a description, offered in whatever words came; an LLM
embedding receives such words and returns a place.

Here one further choice opens: which words are embedded decides what the bundle
carries. Embed the nouns given to a work, and its themes enter the content
space; embed the adjectives, and the impression it leaves does.
Section~\ref{subsec:book_library} uses exactly this freedom to give one
collection two kinds of rabbit holes.

%%% Subsection 6.2 %%%
\subsection{A Music Library Built from Sound
    Alone}\label{subsec:music_library}

\paragraph{Setup.}
We build the first library from MTG-Jamendo~\cite{Bogdanov2019}, an openly
licensed music collection. Five genres---classical, jazz, rock, electronic, and
folk---enter the library, with one thousand tracks from each, and every track
carries exactly one of the five genre tags. The content data consist of sound
alone: each track is embedded in its entirety by CLAP, normalized to unit norm,
and reduced by PCA to ten dimensions. Only these ten-dimensional embeddings and
the genre labels are used for modeling. The collection also carries tags of
another kind---instruments, moods, and themes, freely supplied by the
community---but these are held out: none enters the modeling. They return
afterward, to be read against the space the sound itself has built.

\paragraph{The learned bundle.}
Figure~\ref{fig:mtg_otfb} shows what the optimal transport adds. The
five galaxies separate cleanly in the content space $\mathcal{X}$:
sound alone, embedded, already organizes the collection by genre. In
(a), a manifold is fitted to each galaxy independently: each fits
its own galaxy well, and each curls in a direction of its own. In
(b), the five manifolds are learned jointly as one OT-FB: the fit is
comparable, but the five are now homologous---one sheet, deforming
as it travels from galaxy to galaxy. The difference is not in how
well each floor fits its works; it is in whether the floors agree on
a coordinate. Fitting alone leaves the correspondence arbitrary; the
transport cost chooses one.

The choice becomes visible when the manifolds are stacked as floors.
In (c), the lines cross and tangle; in (d), the same lines, now the
rabbit holes, descend in order from floor to floor. The two stacks
show what each assumption does. Both fit their galaxies, and in both
the coordinate $v$ runs independently of the genre: (A1) alone gives
this much. But under (A1) alone, no information about the coordinate
passes between the floors, so a shared coordinate cannot arise: each
$v$ answers to its own galaxy, and the same $v$ on two floors names
no common place. The proximity (A2) supplies the exchange. Through
the transport cost, the five manifolds are smoothed into the single
map $\Phi$ (Section~\ref{sec:computation}), and in the smoothing they
become homologous: the floors align with the least total movement,
each genre matched to the others as a whole, not work by nearest work
(Section~\ref{subsec:two_genres}).

\paragraph{Reading the space through the tags.}
What, then, does the connotation space $\mathcal{V}$ express? We
examine this through the tags held out of the modeling---the
instruments, moods, and themes the community gave the tracks. Each
tag defines a density over the space: the distribution
$p(v \mid \textit{tag})$ of the works that carry the tag. Where
these works fall evenly, $\mathcal{V}$ carries nothing of the tag;
where they concentrate, the tag tells us what that region holds.

Figure~\ref{fig:tag_density} shows the distributions of four
representative tags: piano, drums, acoustic guitar, and happy. Each
concentrates in a region of its own---piano in the lower left, drums
in the upper right, acoustic guitar in the lower right, happy in the
upper left. And each spreads beyond its region, overlapping the
others: the works are arranged not in discrete islands, one per tag,
but along a continuous spectrum, over which the tags shade into one
another.

\paragraph{The landscape of $\mathcal{V}$.}
Figure~\ref{fig:tag_peaks} widens the reading to forty
representative tags, each placed at the peak of its distribution
$p(v \mid \textit{tag})$. Around every peak the distribution
spreads, as in Figure~\ref{fig:tag_density}; the connotation space
$\mathcal{V}$ is the spectrum into which these overlapping
distributions blend.

The landscape divides in two. The upper half is where the music is
lively: to the right, drums, bass, electric guitar, and
brass---powerful music; to the left, happy and uplifting music. The
lower half is where the music is quiet: to the right, acoustic
guitar, with love and dream---ballads; to the left, piano, with
emotional, relaxing, sad, and romantic. The two coordinates of
$\mathcal{V}$ carry no names, no more than a principal component
does; yet the landscape they span is legible from end to end.

In this landscape, some tags are strongly expressed and others
hardly at all. How much of a tag $\mathcal{V}$ expresses is measured
by the mutual information $\mathrm{MI}[v; \textit{tag}]$: large when
the works carrying the tag fall unevenly over the space, zero when
they fall evenly. Table~\ref{tab:tag_mi} lists the top twenty tags.
Instruments occupy the upper ranks, and moods follow. This is what
the principal connotation space of this collection means: the
spectrum of music that these tags describe in combination.

\paragraph{What a $v$ holds.}
What connotation, then, does a single point $v$ hold? We read four
points---the corners A--D of the map, each of them a rabbit
hole---through the tags concentrated there, listed in
Table~\ref{tab:point_tags}. Point A holds a spectrum of works played
by powerful instruments---brass, bongo, trombone---with the moods
fast and energetic and the themes sport and party. In contrast,
point D holds a spectrum of piano works that are relaxing, romantic,
sad, and emotional. Likewise, B is a spectrum of ballads and love
songs by acoustic guitar and voice, and C a spectrum of funny works
by accordion and ukulele. No point is described by a single word;
yet from the tags together, the reader can imagine what connotation
each point expresses.

\paragraph{What the connotation space represents.}
The space $\mathcal{V}$ was built from the sound and the genre
labels alone; none of these tags took part in the modeling. The tags
then read what the sound had built: a continuous spectrum of music,
legible as a landscape in the large, and at every point as a
spectrum of works. The names arrived afterward, and found their
places already prepared. This is the unnamed vocabulary of
Section~\ref{subsec:bundle_reach}, now seen at work: the space does
not say \textit{emotional} or \textit{hopeful}, but it holds a place
where each can be said.

And this is one space, shared by the five genres: each point of
$\mathcal{V}$ is a rabbit hole, opening at the same $v$ on every
floor. At point A, rock is energetic music driven by drums; leap
through the hole to the classical floor, and there is brass playing
with the same force. At point D, emotional piano jazz leads through
the hole to romantic classical piano---no trumpet, but close to what
the visitor of Section~\ref{sec:introduction} was seeking.

\paragraph{Setup.}
The second library is built from a collection of 409 well-known
children's books. The content data are two descriptions of each
book: one of its themes and one of the impression it leaves. The
descriptions are generated automatically with an LLM annotator
(OpenAI GPT-4): for each book, the annotator chooses fitting words
from two controlled vocabularies---74 theme words
(\textit{friendship}, \textit{courage}, \textit{loss}) and 79
impression words (\textit{humorous}, \textit{poignant},
\textit{cozy}), six to eight of each---and the chosen words are
joined into a description by the recipe of
Section~\ref{subsec:content_data}: ``\textit{This is a children's
    book. The impressions are humorous, warm, and adventurous}.'' The
two descriptions are embedded separately by an LLM text encoder
(OpenAI \texttt{text-embedding-3-small}), normalized to unit norm,
and reduced by PCA to ten dimensions. One collection thus yields
two content datasets: theme data and impression data. The
descriptors are genres, fifteen in all, assigned likewise by the
annotator, and they arrive fuzzy: each book names a primary and a
secondary genre, sometimes a third, each with a strength---fantasy
above all, adventure next. This is the case the first extension of
Section~\ref{sec:computation} anticipated: genre-ness becomes
continuous, and the floor coordinate becomes a per-work coordinate
$u_n$.

\paragraph{One base, two bundles.}
The library is built in two stages. First the base: the genre
descriptors, verbalized with their strengths and embedded in the
same way, determine the denotation coordinates $u_n$.
Figure~\ref{fig:book_otfb}(a) shows the learned genre map: the two
dramas side by side, the two fantasies adjoining legends, myths, and
folktales, adventure beside science fiction and horror. Then, over
this one base, the two bundles are learned: one from the theme
space and one from the impression space. Every book now holds one
denotative address and two connotative addresses,
$(u, v_{\mathrm{thm}}, v_{\mathrm{imp}})$---and under every book,
two rabbit holes open.

\paragraph{The two landscapes.}
Let us see what connotation spaces are organized. Figures~\ref{fig:book_otfb}(b)
and (c) read the two spaces as in Figure~\ref{fig:tag_peaks}: of the words used
in the descriptions, the thirty that carry the most information about $v$---the
highest in mutual information---are placed where the books carrying each word
appear most. The connotation at a point of $\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{thm}}$ or
$\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{imp}}$ is understood as the spectrum of the words
spreading around it.

The theme landscape is legible from end to end:
bonds and inner life in the upper left---family, friendship,
empathy, loss, healing; struggle in the upper right---war, courage,
hope, oppression, perseverance; the moral order below it---justice,
good-and-evil, magic, greed; and the play of the mind in the lower
left---imagination, curiosity, discovery, learning,
problem-solving. The impression landscape divides along other
lines: sorrow above---melancholic, poignant, bittersweet, haunting;
tension below---mysterious, suspenseful, thrilling; warmth in the
upper right---gentle, cozy, heartwarming, nostalgic; play in the
lower right---whimsical, playful, humorous. The same 409 books are
arranged twice: once by what they are about, once by the impression
they leave.

The transport stands behind these landscapes. The OT-FB extracts a connotation
shared across the genres: the coordinate $u$ carries what belongs to a genre,
and $v$ retains what the genres have in common
(Section~\ref{subsec:bundle_reach}). The words that rank high in mutual
information are therefore the words whose nuance travels across the genres;
words specific to a genre are less informative in these spaces. This is the
optimal transport at work.

\paragraph{Which hole to take.}
Table~\ref{tab:book_holes} takes the leap. Three books stand on the
same floor, Drama-Daily, and under each of them the two holes open;
the middle column lists the words concentrated at each hole. Through
a theme hole, the books met on the other floors share the subject of
the book left behind; through an impression hole, they share the
impression it leaves. And a leap is not the end of the exploration:
hopping off on a floor, the visitor walks it as before, among the
books arranged around the hole. The same book, two holes, two
journeys.

\paragraph{The visitor's choice.}
The remark that closed Section~\ref{subsec:bundle_reach} is now an
actual library. The connotation coordinate is determined by the
content data, and this collection carries two: describe the books by
what they are about, and one bundle of holes is dug; describe them
by the impression they leave, and another. Neither is \emph{the}
connotation space of the collection---each is the principal
connotation space of one way of speaking about the works. The
visitor is no longer asked whether to leap; he is asked which hole
to take.
