\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What the Facet-Induced Connotation Space
    Means}\label{subsec:what_it_means}

We have built the space and read it. What does it mean? The machine's answer
is exact: the space holds the simplest structure the facet leaves behind---%
simplest in the sense of Occam's razor, and principal in the sense of PCA.
Linear algebra says it as a picture. Meaning spans a space of infinite
dimension; the words we hold span a finite subspace of it; a complement
therefore always remains, orthogonal to everything the words account for. Our
space is the principal part of that complement. Without a basis, no
complement: the named is what locates the unnamed.

And to a person? If the content space is faithful (Axiom~1), the simplest
remaining structure is smooth, and such a simple structure is likely to be a
qualia spectrum that many people feel in common. Likely, and no more than
likely: Occam's razor promises this much, and no more. And in
Section~\ref{sec:demonstration} it held---instruments and moods in the music
library, the emotions of viewers in the painting library. Whether people do
find meaning there is answered by watching them---by asking whether this space
changes how they walk the library. That is the next stage.



\subsection{Reading Connotation from
    Preference}\label{subsec:reading_preference}

\paragraph{Preference data as content data.}
Content data need not describe a work. The OT-FB asks one thing of them: that
proximity between works be measurable (Section~\ref{subsec:content_space}).
Preference data gathered from enough people meet this condition; recommender
systems rest on exactly such data~\citep{Koren200930}. They say nothing about
any work directly---they say what people chose. Yet where the proximity they
give agrees with the proximity the content data give, the connotation space that
follows is the same space. Connotation lies in the relations among works, and
equally in the relations between works and people.

Preference data also let each user be weighed. $\mathrm{MI}[v;
        \textit{user}]$ measures how much a user tells about the connotation---the
same measure that read the tags in Section~\ref{subsec:music_library}. A user
whose favorites run across genres tells much; a user who stays within one genre
tells little.

\paragraph{The rabbit hole as anthologist.}
Users highly informative about connotation have a name in the world:
anthologists, who wander across genres and gather works by a criterion of their
own~\citep{Riva2017}. So $\mathrm{MI}[v; \textit{user}]$ measures
\emph{anthologist-ness}. And anthologists---on the web, in the world of
criticism---have long joined field to field: living rabbit holes. A rabbit hole
plays their very role, then, and a library with rabbit holes is a library with
librarians and anthologists.

Return, then, to the reference desk of
Section~\ref{sec:introduction}. The visitor asks for ``something
like Miles Davis''; the librarian serves everything a name can
reach---the recordings, the genre, the trumpeters---and where names
end, she does not stop: she calls the anthologists. Who among them
holds Miles Davis dear? One steps forward, one whose gatherings run
through jazz and far beyond it, and shows how to leap, as the white
rabbit did; the visitor leaps. What once waited weeks for a street
pianist now stands at the end of a corridor.
