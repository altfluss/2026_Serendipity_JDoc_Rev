\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What the Facet-Induced Connotation Space
    Means}\label{subsec:what_it_means}
We built the space, and we read it. What does it mean?

In information-theoretic terms the answer is exact. The facet carries part
of the information in the content data, and $\mathcal{V}$ holds the rest.
The OT bias makes $v$ independent of $u$, and among all such residues
$\mathcal{V}$ takes the simplest geometrical form: simple in Occam's sense,
principal in the sense of PCA.

Linear algebra gives a metaphor for this. Let meaning span a space of
infinite dimension, and the words we hold span a subspace of finite
dimension. Connotation always remains outside that subspace. To reach it we
need one more basis, orthogonal to the subspace of names. That basis gives
us the principal part of the complement. Without names, there is nothing to
be orthogonal to. The OT-FB finds such a basis, and it spans the
facet-induced connotation space.

For people the answer is weaker, and only people can give it. Axioms 1 and 2
make the ideal content space faithful to what people feel. There, the simplest
structure left is smooth, and by Axiom 3 a smooth structure is a
quale---likely one many people share. Instrument, mood, and emotion were such
qualia. Occam's razor promises no more, and real embeddings only as far as
they approach the ideal. Either way, one thing holds. People laid the facet;
the content data itself raised the space upon it.

The library now stands, so the question can now be put: does a rabbit hole
change how a visitor walks? That answer waits for visitors.



\subsection{Reading Connotation from
    Preference}\label{subsec:reading_preference}

\paragraph{Preference data as content data.}
The OT-FB asks one thing of content data: that closeness between works be
measurable (Section~\ref{subsec:content_space}). Description is not required.
Preference data gathered from enough people meets this condition, and
recommender systems already stand on such data~\citep{Koren200930}. Preference
data speaks of people rather than of works: it says what people chose. Yet where
its closeness agrees with the closeness of content data, the connotation space
that follows is the same space. Connotation lives in the relation among works,
and equally in the relation between a work and a person.

Preference data also weighs each user. $\mathrm{MI}[v; \textit{user}]$
measures how much a user tells about connotation, by the same measure used
for the tags in Section~\ref{subsec:music_library}. A user whose favorites
cross genres tells much; a user who stays within one genre tells little.

\paragraph{The rabbit hole as anthologist.}
A user high in this information already has a name in the world:
\textit{anthologist}. Anthologists wander across genres and gather works by a
criterion of their own~\citep{Riva2017}. So $\mathrm{MI}[v; \textit{user}]$
measures \emph{anthologist-ness}. And anthologists---on the web, in the world of
criticism---have long joined field to field. A gathering that crosses genres at
one connotation is a section: they thread the weft by hand. A rabbit hole does
what they have always done, then, and a library with rabbit holes is a library
with librarians and anthologists.

Return, then, to the reference desk of
Section~\ref{sec:introduction}. The visitor asks for ``something
like Miles Davis''; the librarian serves everything a name can
reach---the recordings, the genre, the trumpeters---and where names
end, she does not stop: she calls the anthologists. Who among them
holds Miles Davis dear? One steps forward, one whose gatherings run
through jazz and far beyond it, and shows how to leap, as the White
Rabbit did. The visitor leaps. What once waited weeks for a street
pianist now stands at the end of a corridor.
