\begin{abstract}\noindent
    Serendipity cannot be searched for: no one can name in advance what would count as finding it. What one can do is explore, as visitors to a library do. This paper asks what an information space must provide if serendipity is to be sought, and answers with a concept: a multi-floor library with rabbit holes. In this library, \textit{denotation}, such as genre, determines the floor to which a work belongs, while \textit{connotation}, which cannot be captured by a single word, arranges works within each floor. A \textit{rabbit hole} is a vertical line through the same position on every floor, carrying the same connotation. Users may leap through a hole onto another floor, venturing into new worlds; whether they have serendipitous encounters there is left to them. Constructing such a space is not a matter of interface design, but of information \textit{representation}: it requires a \textit{disentangled} representation separating denotative and connotative aspects. We show that optimal transport provides a natural inductive bias for this disentanglement, and that the resulting library space forms an optimal-transport fiber bundle (OT-FB), a ``bundle of rabbit holes,'' uniquely determined from the collection data. The OT-FB provides users not only with a denotative exploration space but also with what we call the \textit{principal connotation space}, whose points correspond to the rabbit holes. Each work thus receives denotative and connotative coordinates locating it within the overall arrangement of works. Such signposts are what users need: in this space, what cannot be searched for can be sought.
\end{abstract}

\begin{quote}\parindent=0pt\parskip=.5em\noindent
    \begin{center}
        \textbf{Structured Abstract}
    \end{center}
    \textbf{Purpose.}
    Serendipity cannot be searched for: what would count as finding it cannot be named in advance. What remains is exploration. This paper asks what an information space must provide if serendipity is to be sought, and frames the problem as one of information \textit{representation} rather than interface design.

    \textbf{Design/methodology/approach.}
    We model the exploration space as a multi-floor library with \textit{rabbit holes}, built from a \textit{disentangled representation} of collection data. Denotation, such as genre, determines the floor to which a work belongs, while connotation arranges works within each floor. A rabbit hole connects the same connotative position across floors, allowing users to move deliberately between denotatively different regions.

    \textbf{Findings.}
    We show that optimal transport provides a natural inductive bias for this representation. The resulting \textit{optimal-transport fiber bundle (OT-FB)}, uniquely determined from the collection data, yields both a denotative exploration space and a \textit{principal connotation space}, whose points correspond to rabbit holes. Principal connotation is determined not by pairwise similarity alone, but by how denotative groups relate to one another as wholes.

    \textbf{Originality/value.}
    The paper shifts the design of serendipitous exploration from interface to representation, gives the required disentangled representation a definite mathematical form, and derives a connotation space from the collection itself. The result is an information space in which users can intentionally enter new worlds without predetermining what they will find: what cannot be searched for can be sought.
\end{quote}
