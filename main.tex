\begin{abstract}\noindent
    Serendipity cannot be searched for: no one can name in advance what would
    count as finding it. What one can do is explore, as visitors to a library
    always have. This paper asks what an information space must hold if
    serendipity is to be sought in it, and answers with a concept: a library
    with rabbit holes. Its floors gather works under a named descriptor such as
    genre; between them run \textit{rabbit holes}, passages that cross the names
    entirely while holding fixed a commonality no such name carries. An explorer
    can leap through a rabbit hole into another world whenever they want, with a
    chance of finding something serendipitous there. Such doors belong to
    \textit{representation}, not interface design---no interface can surface
    what the underlying representation does not already encode. Separating the
    named from the unnamed is ill-posed: infinitely many representations fit the
    same data, and no computation can choose among them. An \textit{inductive
        bias} must declare what one wants, and it cannot itself be a naming. We
    propose \textit{optimal transport} as that bias. Once declared, the
    resulting \textbf{Optimal Transport Fiber Bundle (OT-FB)} is well-defined
    and essentially unique, with a closed-form embedding; learned from 2,500
    songs across ten genres, it yields an atmosphere coordinate common to all
    genres yet independent of any. The contribution is the representation, and
    the account it carries: designing an exploration space holds an irreducible
    act of declaration---one for documentation, not computation.
\end{abstract}

\begin{quote}\parindent=0pt\parskip=.5em\noindent
    \begin{center}
        \textbf{Structured Abstract}
    \end{center}
    \textbf{Purpose.}
    Serendipity cannot be searched for: no one can name in advance what would
    count as finding it. What one can do is explore. This paper asks what an
    information space must hold if serendipity is to be sought in it, and
    answers with a concept: a library with \textit{rabbit holes}---a question of
    \textit{representation}, not interface design.

    \textbf{Design/methodology/approach.}
    Floors gather works under a named descriptor such as genre; rabbit holes
    cross the names while holding fixed a commonality none of them carries.
    Separating the two is ill-posed: infinitely many representations fit the
    same data. An \textit{inductive bias} must declare what one wants---and
    cannot itself be a naming. We propose the \textit{optimal transport (OT)
        hypothesis}, yielding the \textbf{Optimal Transport Fiber Bundle
        (OT-FB)}. It is self-contained at three levels: conceptual, mathematical
    (well-definedness, essential uniqueness, a closed-form embedding), and
    computational (learning from works and descriptors).

    \textbf{Findings.}
    Declaring the hypothesis determines the bundle: well-defined and essentially
    unique. Learned from 2,500 songs across ten genres of the Million Song
    Dataset, it yields an atmosphere coordinate common to all genres yet
    independent of any---organized by tags that cross genres, not by genre-bound
    ones.

    \textbf{Originality/value.}
    The contribution is the representation itself: an unnamed affinity given
    definite geometric form without being named, and learnable from real data.
    With it comes an account of why serendipity's felt closeness resists
    reduction to named attributes, and of what a representation must hold
    instead. Designing an exploration space holds an irreducible act of
    declaration---one for documentation, not computation.
\end{quote}
