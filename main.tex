\begin{abstract}\noindent
    Serendipity cannot be searched for: no one can name in advance what would
    count as finding it. What one can do is explore, as library visitors do.
    This paper asks what an information space must provide if serendipity is to
    be sought, and answers with a concept: a multi-floor library with rabbit
    holes. In this library, denotative attributes, such as genre, decide a
    work's floor, while connotative ones, which no name gathers, arrange the
    works on it. A \textit{rabbit hole} then runs vertically through the same
    connotative position on every floor, so that users may leap from a work that
    resonates into another genre, and there seek serendipity. Such a library, we
    show, can be realized as a \textit{fiber bundle} whose correspondence is
    fixed by optimal transport. This amounts to a decomposition: from the
    generic representation space, a \textit{denotative space} is separated out,
    and beside it stands the \textit{principal connotative space}, a subspace of
    the orthogonal complement supplying what the denotative space cannot. The
    resulting \textit{optimal transport fiber bundle} (OT-FB), determined
    essentially uniquely by the data, gives every work a denotative and a
    connotative coordinate. In this geometry, serendipity would be understood as
    an encounter with a work denotatively distant and connotatively close, and
    the insight that visits the user as the rabbit hole itself. What the paper
    offers, therefore, is twofold: a library with rabbit holes, realized as a
    geometry; and a formulation of connotation and serendipity, which no name
    reaches, as the geometry of an information representation space.
\end{abstract}

\begin{quote}\parindent=0pt\parskip=.5em\noindent
    \begin{center}
        \textbf{Structured Abstract}
    \end{center}
    \textbf{Purpose.}
    Serendipity cannot be searched for---what would count as finding it cannot
    be named in advance---so what remains is exploration. This paper asks what
    an information space must provide if serendipity is to be sought, and frames
    it as a problem of representation, not interface design.

    \textbf{Design/methodology/approach.}
    We model the exploration space as a multi-floor library with \textit{rabbit
        holes}, built from a \textit{disentangled representation} of collection
    data. Denotative attributes, such as genre, decide the floor; connotative
    ones arrange the works on it. A rabbit hole joins the same connotative
    position across floors, letting users leap from a work that resonates into
    another genre.

    \textbf{Findings.}
    Such a library is realized as a \textit{fiber bundle}, its correspondence
    fixed by optimal transport. This decomposes the generic representation space
    into a \textit{denotative space} and the \textit{principal connotative
        space}, a subspace of the orthogonal complement supplying what
    denotation cannot. The resulting \textit{optimal transport fiber bundle}
    (OT-FB), essentially unique given the data, gives every work a denotative
    and a connotative coordinate. Connotative position follows from how
    denotative groups stand as wholes, rather than from pairwise similarity.

    \textbf{Originality/value.}
    The paper recasts connotation and serendipity as geometry. A connotation is
    a quality shared by works that no name gathers; its coordinate stands in for
    the name. Serendipity would then be understood as an encounter with a work
    denotatively distant and connotatively close, and the insight that visits
    the user as the rabbit hole itself. An exploration space for the unnamed
    thus becomes definable and buildable.
\end{quote}
